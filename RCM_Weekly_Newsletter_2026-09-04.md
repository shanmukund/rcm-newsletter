## Section 01 — Regulatory / Payment Updates

### Ten Days Left on the Last Open Comment Clock — and 119 Days Until Medicare Makes Two-Sided Risk Mandatory for Cardiology and Low Back Pain in 235 Markets

Two rulemaking clocks were running when this newsletter last published. One has since expired. The **CY 2027 OPPS/ASC proposed rule (CMS-1850-P)** closed for comment on **August 31, 2026** — four days ago. Whatever your practice did or did not file on the Inpatient-Only list, the ASC Covered Procedures List, and the proposed 340B repricing to ASP−33.4%, that door is now shut until the final rule appears in November.

One clock is still open, and it is the bigger one. The **CY 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule proposed rule (CMS-1848-P)**, released July 14, closes for comment on **September 14, 2026** — **ten days from today**. It is the last opportunity to influence a rule that changes how nearly every physician service in Medicare is priced.

**Key figures:**

| Metric | CY 2027 proposed | Change from CY 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Conversion factor — qualifying APM participants | **$33.1693** | −$0.40 / **−1.19%** |
| Conversion factor — all other clinicians | **$32.8409** | −$0.56 / **−1.68%** |
| Statutory update — QP / non-QP | +0.75% / +0.25% | — |
| Work RVU budget-neutrality adjustment | +0.53% | — |
| Expiration of the one-year WFTC increase | **−2.50%** | Applies to both CFs |

The arithmetic is worth stating plainly: CMS is applying a small positive statutory update on top of a 2.5% cliff. The one-year payment increase Congress provided for CY 2026 expires December 31, and nothing in this rule replaces it. That is the entire explanation for how a "+0.75% update" produces a **−1.19%** conversion factor.

### The Four Proposals That Change Practice Economics

**1. The modifier 25 stacking cut.** When the same physician — or another physician in the same group — furnishes a separately identifiable office or outpatient E/M visit on the same day as a procedure carrying a 0-, 10-, or 90-day global period, Medicare would pay the **most expensive service at 100% and every other service on that claim at 50%**. CMS's stated rationale is overlapping physician work and practice expense. CMS is explicitly soliciting comment on whether **25%** would be the more accurate reduction — which is the single most answerable question in the entire rule, and the one where a specialty-specific comment letter has the clearest path to changing a number.

**2. G2211 stops being a code and becomes a modifier.** Rather than a flat-dollar add-on, the complexity add-on would convert to a **two-digit modifier that increases the base E/M payment by 16%** — the same percentage at every visit level. A second modifier, restricted to clinicians in the Medicare Shared Savings Program and the LEAD Model, would increase qualifying E/M payment by **32%**.

**3. Practice expense methodology.** CMS proposes to phase out its reliance on specialty-specific PE/hour data collected in **2007 or earlier**, replacing it with a "PE stabilizer" intended to damp volatility without anchoring to a two-decade-old benchmark.

**4. Remote monitoring tightens.** RTM would be limited to **established patients only**, would require a separately billable initiating visit, and would have to be delivered by **employed staff rather than contractors** — a direct hit to the outsourced-RPM-vendor model that many small practices adopted precisely because they could not staff it internally.

CMS also proposes extending in-person visit waivers for mental health services in RHCs and FQHCs, and for non-behavioral telehealth, **through December 31, 2027**, aligning the regulatory calendar with the statutory telehealth extension enacted in February.

### The Deadline No One Can Comment Their Way Out Of

While the comment window occupies everyone's attention, a **finalized** rule is already 119 days from taking effect. The **Ambulatory Specialty Model (ASM)** launches **January 1, 2027** — a mandatory, two-sided risk alternative payment model for specialists treating Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries with **heart failure** and **low back pain** in outpatient settings.

- **235 core-based statistical areas** and metropolitan divisions were selected — roughly **25% of the country**
- **Participation is mandatory.** There is no opt-out and no hardship exemption pathway
- Five performance years, **2027–2031**, with payment years **2029–2033**
- Scoring across four MIPS-derived categories — Quality, Cost, Improvement Activities, Interoperability — benchmarked against regional peers
- Payment adjustments range from **−9% to +9%**

The specialties pulled in are cardiology on the heart failure side, and pain management, interventional pain, neurosurgery, orthopedic surgery, and physical medicine and rehabilitation on the low back pain side.

> **⚠️ Warning:** A solo cardiologist practicing in one of the 235 selected CBSAs will be in a mandatory two-sided risk model on January 1, 2027, with a ±9% swing on Medicare Part B revenue and no application to submit, no hardship exemption to request, and no opt-out to file. If you have not checked your CBSA against the ASM participant list, that is the highest-consequence unopened item on your desk this week.

## Section 02 — Prior Authorization / Payer Relations

### The September 1 Payer Wave Lands: UnitedHealthcare Caps Five Lab Tests, Aetna Docks Radiology 15% Without "Smart Dose," BCBS Michigan Starts Dismantling Incident-To

September 1 is the busiest single payer effective date of the second half of the year, and this one was heavier than usual. Six payers moved policy inside a four-day window, and the changes are not administrative housekeeping — three of them change what a practice gets paid for work it is already doing.

| Payer | Effective | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| **UnitedHealthcare** | **Sept 1** (commercial, exchange, MA); staggered Aug–Dec for Medicaid | Five new lab policies: allergen testing capped at **20 allergens per year** for patients 20+; hepatic fibrosis testing limited to **once per six months** for HBV, HCV, MASLD, or alcoholic hepatitis; **non-reimbursement** for in vitro chemotherapy sensitivity and resistance assays; testosterone testing excludes asymptomatic patients; vitamin B12 capped at **once per three months** |
| **Aetna** | **Sept 1** | Expanded Claim and Code Review Program with new claim edits; **15% payment reduction** for radiology services billed with modifier CT that do not meet "Smart Dose" standards |
| **BCBS Michigan** | **Sept 1** (Phase 1); **March 1, 2027** (Phase 2) | Phase 1: non-physician providers billing incident-to must append **modifier SA**; claims still pay at the clinician rate but become ineligible for incentive programs. Phase 2: direct billing required for clinicians with their own NPI, incident-to claims drop to **80% of the professional fee**, and training-level clinicians lose office incident-to eligibility |
| **BCBS Texas** | **Sept 4** | **24 lab-related policies** updated — flow cytometry, PSA, allergen, hepatitis, thyroid, cardiovascular, micronutrient, drug testing, colorectal cancer screening |
| **BCBS North Carolina** | **Sept 1** | Multiple/bilateral surgery restructured — outpatient facility: 100% / 50% / 50% / **0% on the fourth and beyond**; professional: 100% / 50% / **25% on the third and beyond** |
| **Molina Healthcare of Ohio** | **Sept 1** (Medicaid) | New specialty medication site-of-care policy, gene therapy coverage for Otarmeni, revised buy-and-bill policies for multiple specialty drugs, updated duplicate-claims payment integrity policy |

### The Michigan Change Is the One to Read Twice

Incident-to billing has been an economic assumption underneath NP and PA staffing models for two decades. BCBS Michigan is not eliminating it in one step — it is doing it in two, and Phase 1 is deliberately painless so that Phase 2 arrives inside an existing workflow. Appending modifier SA costs nothing on September 1. On **March 1, 2027**, the same claim, with the same modifier, pays **80%**.

Any practice modeling NP/PA contribution margin on 100% incident-to reimbursement should re-run that model now, not in February. And the pattern matters more than the payer: when one Blues plan phases out incident-to economics, the others read the results.

> **✅ Action Required:** Pull your top 20 lab CPT codes and check them against the new UnitedHealthcare frequency caps before the first denial batch posts. Allergen panels above 20 units per year, B12 more often than quarterly, and hepatic fibrosis testing inside six months will now deny — and a frequency denial after the fact costs far more to appeal than an order-set edit costs to build.

### The Federal Clock: 119 Days

The **CMS-0057-F** attestation deadline in HPMS passed on **September 1** — three days ago. That was the payer-side checkpoint. The provider-side consequence arrives **January 1, 2027 — 119 days from today**, when impacted payers must operate four FHIR APIs: **Patient Access, Provider Access, Payer-to-Payer, and Prior Authorization**.

The Prior Authorization API is the one that changes daily work. It must support checking whether authorization is required, surfacing the specific documentation needed, electronic submission, and electronic receipt of the decision — all from inside the EHR or practice management system. The 2026 operational requirements are already live: **72 hours for expedited** and **seven calendar days for standard** decisions, specific denial reasons, and public reporting of approval rates, denial rates, appeal outcomes, and average decision times.

Separately, the **WISeR model** continues in its ninth month, applying prior authorization or pre-payment review to **13 service categories** — including epidural steroid injections, cervical fusion, and skin substitutes — for Traditional Medicare patients in **New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas, Arizona, and Washington**, across four MAC jurisdictions. The model runs through **December 31, 2031**.

## Section 03 — AI & Automation Deep Dive

### Autonomous Coding Is Now 44.8% of the AI-Coding Market and 97% Accurate in Production — 27 Days Before a 379-Code Retraining Event

Autonomous coding spent five years as the most-promised and least-deployed technology in the revenue cycle. The 2026 market data says that phase is over.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Global AI-in-medical-coding market, 2026 | **$3.86 billion** |
| Projected 2034 | **$10.84 billion** (13.76% CAGR) |
| Autonomous coding tools — share of that market in 2026 | **44.8%** |
| Autonomous coding tools — segment CAGR | **36.74%** |
| Healthcare providers — share of end-user market | **46.23%** |
| North America — share of global medical coding market, 2026 | **38.8%** |

The accuracy numbers have moved with the market. Current production deployments report **98.5% E/M accuracy**, **98.5% CPT and modifier accuracy**, and **95.5% ICD accuracy**, with autonomous coding rates up to **85%** and direct-to-bill rates above **85%** at roughly 95% accuracy without human intervention. R1 RCM reports agentic AI applications in production with select clients, including autonomous coding running at accuracy rates **as high as 97%** for service lines such as emergency room and physician office visits. Nym Health and Fathom remain the reference implementations, with Fathom the first autonomous coder in Epic's App Orchard.

### The Question to Ask Your Vendor This Month

Here is the part the market data does not cover, and it is the reason this section is running 27 days before October 1 rather than in January.

**An autonomous coder is a trained model.** On October 1, **379 new codes** enter production across ICD-10-CM, ICD-10-PCS, and HCPCS Level II, with **68 deletions** alongside them. A human coder handles that with a training session and a reference card. A model handles it only if someone retrained it — and only if its fallback behavior on an unmapped or newly-deleted code routes the encounter to a human queue instead of guessing at the nearest neighbor.

Three questions, in writing, to every autonomous coding and CDI vendor you use:

1. **What is your FY 2027 model refresh date**, and will it be in production before October 1 or after?
2. **What is the fallback behavior** when the model encounters a code deleted effective October 1, or a clinical concept that maps only to a new code?
3. **What is your confidence-threshold policy during the transition** — does the human-review rate rise automatically in October, and by how much?

A vendor that cannot answer question 2 has told you something important.

### Meanwhile, Prior Authorization Automation Has Its Receipts

The clearest ROI in the automation stack this year is not coding — it is prior authorization, where the results are now specific enough to hold a vendor to:

- **68% of all orders cleared within one hour** and nearly **97% within one day** under R1's prior authorization product, with an average auth-related denial rate **below 1%**
- A Florida health system reports **more than 99%** of prior authorizations cleared within three days
- **WPS Health Solutions**, on the payer side, cut average processing time for targeted services by **30.27%** by raising its straight-through processing rate and using AI to assist human reviewers
- Teams using voice AI report cutting administrative phone work by **up to 60%**

Against a baseline of **13 hours per week** that a physician and their staff spend on prior authorization for a single physician, those are not incremental numbers.

**The automation stack, top to bottom:**

- **Generative AI** — appeal letter drafting, denial-reason interpretation, documentation gap detection, clinical summarization for medical necessity
- **AI/ML** — autonomous coding, denial prediction, payer behavior modeling, propensity-to-pay scoring
- **RPA** — eligibility checks, claim status polling, payment posting, worklist routing

> **⚠️ Warning:** Do not fund a new AI layer this quarter if your eligibility verification is still manual. Automated eligibility remains the cheapest and highest-yield automation available to a small practice, and every downstream AI investment inherits the error rate of the data underneath it.

## Section 04 — Coding Updates

### 379 New Codes Land on a Single Day: ICD-10-CM (190), ICD-10-PCS (101), and HCPCS Level II (88) All Turn Over October 1

Most years, October 1 is an ICD-10 event. This year all three code sets that CMS controls turn over on the same date, and the HCPCS piece — posted quietly on **August 18** — is the one most practices have not scheduled for.

| Code set | New | Revised | Deleted | Effective | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ICD-10-CM** | **190** | 4 | 30 | Oct 1, 2026 | 33 clinical topics, nearly every chapter |
| **ICD-10-PCS** | **101** | 3 | 38 | Oct 1, 2026 | 50 in New Technology; set totals **79,256** codes |
| **HCPCS Level II** | **88** | — | — | Oct 1, 2026 | Posted Aug 18; 15 requested by individuals or manufacturers |
| **Totals** | **379** | 7 | 68 | **Oct 1, 2026 — 27 days** | |

### ICD-10-CM: Where the 190 Codes Actually Are

They are not evenly distributed, and one category dominates. **Category O31.4 alone contributes 33 new codes** for continuing pregnancy after vanishing twin syndrome of one or more fetuses, organized by trimester and encounter type. Beyond obstetrics:

- **Oncology** — new secondary malignancy codes for metastatic sites including larynx, pharynx, and oral cavity, which previously required nonspecific codes
- **Cardiology** — dilated cardiomyopathy now distinguishes familial and genetic forms; new codes capture arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, CPVT, Brugada syndrome, and ventricular bigeminy
- **Endocrine** — **E89.830** and **E89.838** for hypoglycemia occurring after bariatric and other surgeries
- **BMI** — **Z68.18** (BMI 18.4 or less, adult) and **Z68.19** (BMI 18.5–19.9, adult)
- **Infectious disease** — **Z86.17**, personal history of *Clostridioides difficile* infection
- **Toxicology and public health** — toxic effect of cycloparaffins, and medetomidine codes supporting overdose surveillance as medetomidine appears as an adulterant in the illicit fentanyl supply
- **Rare disease and other** — dental-related sinusitis, VEXAS, Loeys-Dietz, Lynch syndrome

### HCPCS Level II: The 88-Code Update Nobody Calendared

CMS posted the quarterly HCPCS Level II update on **August 18**, effective **October 1**. Among the **88 new codes**, 15 were requested by individuals or manufacturers and finalized by CMS. Notable additions with published fee amounts:

- **A2046** — DermiSphere hDRT composite biodegradable wound dressing, per square centimeter
- **A4228** — supplies for maintenance of a non-insulin device-drug combination infusion catheter, per week; average non-rural fee schedule amount approximately **$124.54**
- **A6614** — supplies and accessories for an external ocular negative pressure pump; 2026 fee schedule amount approximately **$167.99** per unit

CMS is also adding **19 new HCPCS Level II codes** to the home health consolidated billing non-routine supply list effective October 1 — relevant to any practice that bills supplies for patients under a home health episode, because consolidated billing determines who may bill them at all.

### CPT 2027 Publishes This Month

The AMA assigns the actual five-digit numbers when the CPT 2027 code set publishes in **September 2026** — this month. What is already known about January 1, 2027:

- **Maternity care services** are restructured — the first substantive overhaul in roughly 30 years, intended to reflect team-based obstetric care and reporting across the full pregnancy spectrum
- **Nine new codes** for open, laparoscopic, and thoracoscopic **diaphragmatic hernia repair**
- New **Category I** reporting for **endoscopic submucosal dissection** in the upper and lower GI tract
- **92507 is deleted**, replaced by a restructured set of speech-language pathology codes
- **28 new Category III codes** accepted at the September 2025 Editorial Panel meeting, 11 of them for surface mesh representations and applications using digital 3D modeling
- Three new illustrations differentiating simple, intermediate, and complex **wound repair**

> **✅ Action Required:** Run clearinghouse acceptance testing on October 1 dates of service for **all three** code sets — not just ICD-10-CM — before the last week of September. The most common October failure is not a rejected diagnosis code; it is a supply or device HCPCS code that was valid on September 30 and is not on October 1, sitting on an otherwise clean claim.

## Section 05 — Revenue Velocity / KPIs

### Expenses Per Physician Are Up 26.5% Against 9.1% Revenue Growth — Which Is Why 36% of Practices Named Automation Their Top Cost-Cutting Move

The velocity conversation has been a revenue conversation for three years: get the claim out clean, get it paid faster, work the denial. The 2026 MGMA data argues that the binding constraint has moved to the other side of the ledger.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Total expenses per physician vs. pre-pandemic | **+26.5%** |
| Revenue increase over the same period | **+9.1%** |
| Practice leaders reporting increased regulatory burden over three years | **~95%** |
| Practices employing **3+ FTE administrative staff per physician** | **40%** |
| Survey respondents in practices with 20 or fewer physicians | **52%** |

A 17-point gap between expense growth and revenue growth cannot be closed by collecting faster. It gets closed by collecting the same dollars with less labor — which is precisely what practice leaders said they intend to do.

### Where the 2026 Money Is Going

**Biggest new investment in 2026 budgets** (MGMA Stat, October 2025 poll): workforce **37%**, health IT **30%**, revenue cycle **12%**, patient access **12%**, other **9%**.

**Biggest cost-cutting move for 2026:** automation **36%**, process fixes **23%**, hiring freeze or cuts **18%**, outsourcing **14%**, finding new vendors **9%**. Automation and process fixes together account for **more than half** of all responses — the first year that pairing has outweighed headcount reduction.

Adoption is following. **43%** of groups added or expanded AI in 2024; by 2025, AI tools had edged out EHR usability as the top technology priority, and **71%** of practices reported using some form of AI in patient visits. **36%** of practice leaders said they would outsource or automate parts of RCM, with planned investment concentrated in agentic tools for benefits and eligibility, prior authorization, payer analytics, and coding support. RCM vacancies, meanwhile, remain among the hardest roles to fill.

### Where You Should Be — 2026 KPI Targets

| KPI | Industry reality | Your target |
|---|---|---|
| **Days in A/R — solo / 1–3 providers** | 45–55 typical | **≤ 45** |
| **Days in A/R — 4–10 providers** | 40–48 typical | **≤ 40** |
| **Days in A/R — 10+ providers** | 35–45 typical | **≤ 35** |
| **Days in A/R — top performers, any size** | — | **< 25** |
| **Clean claim rate** | Industry benchmark 95% | **98–99%+** |
| **Initial denial rate** | **11.8%** average; 41% of providers above 10% | **≤ 5%** |
| **Medicare Advantage denial rate** | **>17%**, up 4.8% year over year | Track separately from traditional Medicare |
| **Administrative FTE per physician** | 40% of practices at 3+ | **Measure it monthly; trend it down** |
| **A/R over 90 days** | Varies widely | **≤ 15% of total A/R** |

One relationship in that table is worth acting on directly: practices holding a clean claim rate **above 95%** consistently run days in A/R **below 36**. Clean claim rate is the upstream variable. A/R days is the reported one. If you can only fund one measurement discipline this quarter, measure and fix the first.

> **💡 Key Insight:** The KPI that best predicts whether an independent practice survives the next three years is not days in A/R. It is **administrative FTE per physician** — because it is the only metric that captures the expense side of the 26.5%-versus-9.1% gap, and it is the one number that automation is capable of moving.

## Section 07 — Compliance Corner

### September Is the Last Outreach Month: Medicaid Community Engagement Starts January 1 in 43 States and DC

Every compliance deadline in this issue is a paperwork problem except one. The Medicaid community engagement requirement is a **cash flow** problem, and the operational window to prepare for it closes this month.

Under **CMS-2454-IFC**, beginning **January 1, 2027 — 119 days from today** — states must condition Medicaid eligibility for "applicable individuals" on demonstrated community engagement of **80 hours per month**. The requirement reaches non-pregnant adults **aged 19 to 64** who are not entitled to or enrolled in Medicare and who are eligible for or enrolled in the Medicaid adult group or certain section 1115 demonstrations. **43 states and the District of Columbia** cover those populations and will be required to implement.

The timing detail that makes this a September story: states requiring an applicant to demonstrate **one, two, or three months** of community engagement at application had to begin outreach no later than **September, August, or July 2026** respectively. **September 2026 is the final outreach month** for any state on the shortest lookback. Some states — Nebraska among them — have already implemented early.

### Why This Lands on the Revenue Cycle, Not on Compliance

When an enrollee cannot document 80 hours, the state must send a notice of noncompliance and allow **30 calendar days** to demonstrate compliance or that the requirement does not apply. Some portion of that population will not respond in time. For a practice, that produces the single worst pattern in accounts receivable: **a patient who was covered at scheduling, uncovered at the date of service, and discovered at the 835.**

Three controls, all front-end, all automatable:

1. **Real-time eligibility at every touchpoint** — scheduling, reminder, check-in, and again before claim submission. Not once at registration.
2. **Retroactive eligibility recheck on the 30-day cure window.** A patient disenrolled for nonresponse may be reinstated after curing. Do not write the balance to bad debt on day 31.
3. **Financial-assistance and sliding-scale screening triggered by a coverage-lapse flag**, not by a collections letter 90 days later.

### The Rest of the Compliance Calendar

- **Hospital price transparency** enforcement has been live since **April 1, 2026**. The CY 2026 OPPS final rule eliminated the "estimated allowed amount" placeholder, added three data elements derived from **12 to 15 months** of actual claims history — median, 10th percentile, and 90th percentile charges — and requires a **CEO or designated senior official to attest in writing** that posted prices are true, accurate, and complete. CMS issued **10 civil monetary penalties in 2025**, more than double the prior annual pace.
- **TEFCA** participation continues to earn additional points toward the Promoting Interoperability score for hospitals sharing data with public health agencies through the network.
- **42 CFR Part 2** enforcement is tightening for substance use disorder records, adding documentation and consent obligations to behavioral health practices that already carry the highest denial rates in medicine.

> **⚠️ Warning:** Model the revenue exposure now. Take your Medicaid adult-group volume, apply a conservative disenrollment assumption, and multiply by your average charge. That figure is your Q1 2027 self-pay conversion — and unlike a rate cut, it arrives without a comment period, a transition year, or a notice to your practice.

## Section 08 — Independent Practice Watch

### Three Administrative FTEs Per Physician: 40% of Practices Now Staff That Way Just to Answer Payers

There is a number in the MGMA data that describes independent practice economics better than any margin figure: **40% of practices now employ three or more full-time administrative staff per physician**, purely to manage payer rules, audits, appeals, and reporting requirements.

Not per practice. Per physician.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Practices with **3+ administrative FTEs per physician** | **40%** |
| Leaders reporting increased regulatory burden over three years | **~95%** |
| Expense growth per physician vs. pre-pandemic | **+26.5%** |
| Revenue growth over the same period | **+9.1%** |
| Survey respondents in practices of 20 or fewer physicians | **52%** |

That ratio is the mechanism of consolidation. A three-physician group carrying nine administrative FTEs has a fixed-cost base that does not shrink when Medicare cuts the conversion factor 1.68%, and cannot be amortized across the volume a 400-physician group enjoys. Every incremental payer rule widens the gap, because compliance cost scales with the number of rules, not with the number of physicians.

### How This Week's News Lands Differently on a Small Practice

- **The Ambulatory Specialty Model is the harshest item in this issue for an independent specialist.** A large cardiology group in a selected CBSA has an analytics team to model a ±9% adjustment. A two-cardiologist practice in the same CBSA is in the same mandatory two-sided risk model, with the same MIPS-derived scoring, and **no hardship exemption**.
- **The modifier 25 stacking cut targets the procedure-plus-visit practice.** Dermatology, ENT, and podiatry take the largest projected hit — specialties disproportionately organized as small independent groups. The comment window closes September 14, and CMS asked a specific question about 25% versus 50%. That is an unusually answerable ask.
- **G2211's 32% ACO modifier is a reason to reopen the MSSP conversation.** A 16% E/M uplift becomes 32% for Shared Savings Program and LEAD Model participants. For a primary care practice that has repeatedly declined ACO participation on administrative grounds, the arithmetic changed in July.
- **The incident-to unwind removes a staffing assumption, not just a payment.** BCBS Michigan's Phase 2 drops incident-to claims to 80% of the professional fee on March 1, 2027. Practices whose NP and PA contribution margin assumes 100% should re-model now.
- **The 379-code turnover is a vendor question when you have no coding department.** A large system retrains its coders. A 6-physician practice inherits whatever its EMR and clearinghouse shipped — which is why the three vendor questions in Section 03 matter more here than anywhere else.
- **Medicaid community engagement hits the practices with the thinnest cash buffers.** Practices with high Medicaid adult-group mix and under 30 days cash on hand will feel a Q1 2027 self-pay conversion immediately.

### Primary Care

The G2211 redesign is the most consequential primary care payment change in the proposed rule, and the ACO provision is the part that has not been widely modeled. Converting to a **16% modifier** applied uniformly across E/M levels replaces a flat dollar add-on — which advantages higher-level visits and disadvantages lower-level ones relative to today. The second modifier, restricted to **Shared Savings Program and LEAD Model** participants, pays **32%**. CMS is also proposing new codes for **shared medical appointments** and **advance care planning**. Primary care and internal medicine continue to carry the lowest denial rates in medicine at **8–12%**.

### Cardiology

Two changes land simultaneously. First, the **Ambulatory Specialty Model** makes heart failure care a mandatory two-sided risk proposition in **235 CBSAs** starting January 1, 2027, with **−9% to +9%** payment adjustments scored against regional peers on quality, cost, improvement activities, and interoperability. Second, on October 1, FY 2027 ICD-10-CM adds specificity that cardiology documentation is not currently written for: **familial and genetic dilated cardiomyopathy** distinguished from other forms, plus new codes for **arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, CPVT, Brugada syndrome, and ventricular bigeminy**. Cardiac and thoracic surgery show positive impacts in the CY 2027 specialty impact tables.

### Orthopedics

Orthopedics remains the specialty carrying the largest projected downside in the CY 2027 rule at **−7% overall**, and is the only specialty whose overall impact includes a malpractice-RVU cut. It is now also on both sides of the utilization-control apparatus: the **ASM low back pain arm** covers orthopedic surgery, neurosurgery, interventional pain, pain management, and PM&R, while the **WISeR model** already applies prior authorization or pre-payment review to **cervical fusion** and **epidural steroid injections** in six states. Orthopedic denial rates run **14–22%**.

### Oncology

ASCO estimates a **1.5% overall reimbursement cut** for hematology/oncology and a **1.5% increase** for radiation oncology in CY 2027. Separately, the CY 2027 OPPS proposed rule — now closed for comment — would cut 340B drug payment to **ASP−33.4%** effective January 1, 2027, and raise the offsetting nondrug reduction from 0.5% to **3%** for 2027 through 2029. On the coding side, FY 2027 adds **secondary malignancy codes for specific metastatic sites** including larynx, pharynx, and oral cavity that previously required nonspecific codes — better documentation, and better denial defense on medical necessity. Practices should also note UnitedHealthcare's **non-reimbursement of in vitro chemotherapy sensitivity and resistance assays** effective September 1.

### Radiology

The most immediately actionable item in this section. **Aetna, effective September 1**, applies a **15% payment reduction** to radiology services billed with modifier CT that do not meet "Smart Dose" standards compliance. This is a dose-registry and equipment-attestation problem masquerading as a billing problem, and the reduction applies claim by claim. On the fee schedule side, CY 2027 impact tables project roughly **+2%** for diagnostic radiology and nuclear medicine and roughly **+3%** for interventional radiology.

### Gastroenterology

The AGA's read on the CY 2027 proposed rules is that they would **reduce reimbursement for many GI services**, alter complex-care payments, and introduce requirements affecting coding and billing workflow. The offsetting development is a coding win: CPT 2027 establishes **Category I** reporting for **endoscopic submucosal dissection** in both the upper and lower GI tract effective January 1, 2027 — moving ESD out of unlisted and Category III reporting. GI practices should also budget EHR and modifier-logic work for the G2211 replacement.

### Behavioral Health

Behavioral health carries the **highest denial rates in medicine at 20–30%**, which makes every other change in this section secondary to denial management. The stability news is real: the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026, signed February 3, extended Medicare telehealth flexibilities through **December 31, 2027**, and CMS proposes extending the in-person visit waiver for mental health services in RHCs and FQHCs to match. For CY 2027, CMS proposes adding **smoking and tobacco cessation** and **SBIRT** services to the timed behavioral health code upward adjustment in its final transition year, and proposes keeping adaptive behavior codes **97151–97158** on the permanent telehealth list. Countering that: stricter **42 CFR Part 2** enforcement adds consent and documentation obligations.

### OB / Maternal-Fetal Medicine

The largest single block of new ICD-10-CM codes this year belongs to obstetrics — **33 new codes in category O31.4** for continuing pregnancy after vanishing twin syndrome, stratified by trimester and encounter. Ninety-two days later, CPT 2027 restructures **maternity care services** for the first time in roughly three decades, effective January 1, 2027. An OB practice therefore faces two separate, unrelated code changes ninety days apart, and needs two separate testing cycles.
