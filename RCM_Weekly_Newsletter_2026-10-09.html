CMS Finalizes Transparency in Coverage Overhaul for Payers: Quarterly Reporting Replaces Monthly, Executives Must Vouch for Data Accuracy — and the 2027 Fee Schedule Final Rule Is Due in Six Weeks

Two regulatory events define the billing environment heading into year-end. On October 5, 2026, CMS, the Department of Labor, and the Treasury Department jointly finalized the Transparency in Coverage (TiC) Final Rule (CMS 9882-F) — the most significant revision to machine-readable pricing rules since they took effect in July 2022. The rule takes effect December 5, 2026, with data requirements phasing in through 2027. And separately, the CY 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule final rule is on track for release in mid-November, locking in the conversion factor and hundreds of coding policy decisions that practices have been modeling against the July proposal.

What the TiC Final Rule Changes for Billing Teams

The TiC rule primarily governs what payers must publish, but its downstream effects land on practices: when the rate file a payer posts is wrong or outdated, referral decisions, out-of-network disputes, and patient cost estimates are all built on bad data. The key changes:

ChangeOld requirementNew requirement
Reporting frequencyMonthlyQuarterly
File location indicatorNot requiredPlain-text file at website root pointing to MRF location
“Ghost rates”No explicit standardMust reflect actual paid rates; executive attestation required
Out-of-network dataLimitedExpanded OON pricing information required
Prescription drug filesVoluntarySchema development begins November 2026; full requirement follows

The “ghost rate” problem has been the biggest practical failure of the original rules: insurers published negotiated rates that looked real but did not reflect actual payments, either because contracts had changed or because the file included rates for providers who had never treated an insured patient. The new attestation requirement — requiring a plan executive to certify the data — is the mechanism CMS is using to force accountability. Practices that see systematic gaps between what a payer’s MRF says it pays and what it actually remits now have a documented accuracy standard to cite in disputes.

Key Insight

A practice that runs a competitive market analysis using payer MRFs today is likely working from stale or incorrect data. The December 5 effective date gives payers 57 days to begin correcting their files. The first quarterly submission under the new standard falls in early 2027. Build any rate benchmarking or payer negotiation work on the assumption that the first accurate file won’t be available until Q1–Q2 2027.

The Fee Schedule Final Rule: What Is Still Unsettled

The July proposed rule contained four decisions that affect day-to-day billing for most practices and that will be locked in by the November final rule:

  1. Conversion factor: Proposed at $32.8409 for non-qualifying APM participants (down ~1.68% from 2026) and $33.1693 for qualifying APM participants (down ~1.19%). The final number may change modestly but is unlikely to turn positive absent legislation.
  2. G2211 modifier: CMS proposes converting the complexity add-on code into a modifier adding 16% to the value of the billed E/M code, rather than a separate billing unit.
  3. Same-day E/M with global procedures: Proposed 50% reduction for a separately identifiable office E/M billed with a 0-, 10-, or 90-day global procedure on the same day.
  4. MIPS sunset: CMS proposes ending traditional MIPS reporting in 2029, accelerating transition to APMs.
Warning

With federal funding expiring December 11 and the fee schedule final rule expected in November, the last six weeks of 2026 are the highest-density regulatory period practices will face before the January 1, 2027 go-live date for electronic prior authorization, the GLOBE model, and a new conversion factor. Build your 2027 budget line for Medicare revenue before the final rule lands, and revise it within 48 hours of publication.

UHC Week Two: What to Watch for on Remits

UnitedHealthcare’s October 1 change removed prior authorization requirements on approximately 1,700 codes across commercial, Medicare Advantage, Community Plan, Individual Exchange, and Oxford plan lines. The affected clinical areas include cardiology imaging and interventional procedures, oncology, orthopedics, genetic and lab testing, chiropractic, and physical and occupational therapy. For practices that saw repeated PA-related claim delays in these categories through September, last week’s claims should now be cleaner on the prior-auth front.

Two cautions apply. First, removing PA does not remove all claim review: UHC retains post-payment review rights, and a code removed from PA may still trigger concurrent or retrospective utilization management. Second, plan-level variants exist — the authorization requirement for a specific code may differ across Oxford vs. a commercial UHC plan vs. a Medicare Advantage product. Verify each line on the payer’s official code list, not the summary coverage. Fidelis Care also updated its prior authorization requirements effective October 1 per its October 2026 provider bulletin.

The January 1, 2027 Electronic PA Clock

As of January 1, 2027, Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid managed care, and CHIP managed care organizations subject to CMS-0057-F must support the Prior Authorization API — a FHIR-based electronic connection that lets EHR and practice management systems send and receive prior authorization requests without navigating individual payer portals. 61% of practices currently access seven or more payer portals every week (MGMA); the API is designed to collapse that to a single workflow.

The rule requires the payer to build the API; it does not require the provider to use it. A practice whose EHR or clearinghouse has integrated the API by January 1 gets the benefit automatically. Ask your vendor directly about their timeline.

State AI-in-Coverage Decisions: An October 1 Effective Date

Effective October 1, 2026, at least one state (SB 63, signed April 17, 2026) bars health insurers from making coverage determinations based solely on AI and requires disclosure to providers when AI is used in a utilization review decision. The law reflects a growing legislative response to documented cases of automated claim denials upheld without individual clinical review. Similar proposals are active in several additional states. The federal equivalent — a proposed CMS rule requiring disclosure of AI use in coverage decisions for Medicare Advantage plans — remains pending.

Action Required

Before October 15, prepare a one-page internal reference card listing (a) which payer plans in your market are exiting for 2027, (b) which prior authorization codes UHC removed from your highest-volume categories, and (c) when your clearinghouse or EHR vendor plans to support the January 1 electronic PA API. Give it to both your scheduling team and your billing team — open enrollment questions start this week.

How HFMA Maps the Work

The October 6 framework positions three tiers of automation against the revenue cycle workflow:

Gen AI
Appeal letters citing contract language, PA packets, payer correspondence summarization, documentation drafts — high-value but requires human review before send
AI / ML

Agentic / RPA

The Guidehouse/HFMA survey numbers reflect a real split: the 78% using automation skews heavily toward health systems with larger IT budgets and vendor contracts; the 59% who haven’t implemented are disproportionately smaller practices and independent groups.

MGMA’s Starting Point: Revenue Cycle First

MGMA’s IT chief Julia Rosen offered a concrete starting point for practices new to AI: begin in the revenue cycle, not in clinical AI. Denial prediction and coding support are lower regulatory risk than ambient documentation, have faster ROI, and typically live in software the practice already pays for. The guidance echoes a Guidehouse/HFMA theme: the first AI win is usually unlocking a feature inside an existing contract, not buying a new product.

Warning

The 78%/59% data point reflects a measurement problem: organizations that have turned on a single automation feature in their clearinghouse often report “yes” to automation adoption. Implementation maturity matters more than adoption rate. Before benchmarking your AI posture against market numbers, count how many workflows in your existing vendor stack are actually active vs. licensed-but-dormant.

New Hereditary Cancer Syndrome Codes

One of the highest-impact changes for practices running genetic counseling, oncology, GI, or molecular pathology workflows: FY 2027 introduced dedicated ICD-10-CM codes for Lynch syndrome, familial BRCA1/BRCA2 variants, and Li-Fraumeni syndrome — syndromes that previously shared a single “genetic susceptibility to malignant neoplasm” code. The new codes carry their own coverage and medical-necessity rules. A claim using the old unspecified code for a service that now has a specific code available will likely face denial or a documentation request.

The specificity also matters for NCD-governed lab services: any molecular or genetic test tied to a hereditary cancer syndrome diagnosis should now use the specific code to satisfy the LCD/NCD diagnosis pairing.

October 8 MLN: RARC and CARC Updates

The October 8, 2026 MLN Connects edition published updates to the Remittance Advice Remark Codes (RARC) and Claim Adjustment Reason Codes (CARC) — the two-code combination that explains why Medicare denied or adjusted a claim. Any practice using automated denial triage that maps RARC/CARC combinations to workflow routing needs to update its crosswalk. The MLN also reminded providers to confirm their HETTS EDI Enrollment email address by December 1, 2026.

Action Required

Pull all claims submitted October 1 or later and filter for denials that did not appear in September. Group by RARC/CARC pair, then by CPT code. Any cluster involving a code that changed in the October 1 update is a systemic issue — fix the root cause in your charge master or encoder before resubmitting the batch.

The Self-Pay Problem Is Getting Worse

HFMA’s October–November 2026 issue highlights a growing concern: practices deploying patient-facing technology (payment plans, digital statements, cost estimators) are finding it harder to justify those investments purely on short-term financial returns. As 2027 MA plans carry higher maximum out-of-pocket limits and Part D deductibles rise ~30% on average for some plans, the amount patients owe per encounter is rising, but their ability and willingness to pay is not rising proportionally.

The practical response: a pre-service financial clearance workflow that identifies patients with high expected out-of-pocket before the visit and coordinates a payment plan or financial assistance discussion at check-in. Collect $50 before the encounter and document a plan for the rest — rather than chasing a $400 copay in collections 90 days later.

Key Insight

The difference between a 93% and a 96% net collection rate on a $3 million annual revenue base is $90,000. For most independent practices, that gap is closed not by a new vendor but by fixing three or four specific process failures: claims not submitted within 24 hours, self-pay accounts not touched within 30 days, and secondary claims not filed when primary pays. Run that audit before looking at new technology.

The Acquisition Pressure

Veradigm’s March 2026 survey found that 94% of independent practices report being approached about acquisition or consolidation in the past two years. Of those, 26% advanced to serious negotiations before choosing to remain independent. The most significant threats: increased operating costs, administrative and regulatory burden, and reimbursement pressure. Only 24% of practices report clear visibility into where revenue leaks — a foundational gap that makes financial comparisons with acquiring entities difficult.

For practices with more than 40 clinicians, the strain is highest: 82% report increased financial pressure, and the administrative-to-clinical staff ratio approaches that of health system-employed practices without the corresponding infrastructure investment.

The 2027 Site-of-Service Offset

The proposed 2027 fee schedule estimates that its methodology changes would increase Medicare payments for services delivered in independent office-based settings by approximately 4%, while decreasing payments for the same services in facility-based (outpatient hospital or HOD) settings by approximately 7%. This reflects CMS’s ongoing effort to reduce the facility differential that has made hospital employment of physicians economically attractive. The final rule lands in November — confirm whether this offset survives before revising your budget.

Bottom Line

Independence has a material financial value in 2027 if the fee schedule adjustment survives the final rule. The question is whether that value is visible on your P&L — and whether your revenue cycle operations are clean enough to capture it. A practice with a 92% net collection rate on a $3M base loses $90,000 annually compared to a practice at 95%. Fixing that gap is worth more than the site-of-service differential alone.
