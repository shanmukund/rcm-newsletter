## Section 01 — Regulatory / Payment Updates

### Medicare Reprices 1,171 Lab Codes Downward: The Preliminary 2027 Lab Fee Schedule Averages 16% Below 2026, 775 Tests Take the Full 15% Cut — and the Comment Clock Runs Out Around October 21

On **September 21, 2026**, CMS posted the **preliminary CY 2027 Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule (CLFS) rates** — the weighted median private-payer rates that will reset what Medicare pays for clinical diagnostic lab tests starting **January 1, 2027**. The data comes from the second full reporting cycle required under the **Protecting Access to Medicare Act of 2014 (PAMA)**, and CMS’s conclusion is blunt: Medicare has been paying roughly **16% more** for lab services than private payers do.

**Stat cards:**
- **1,171** — Lab codes whose private-payer median came in below the 2026 Medicare rate (of 1,528 with a calculated median)
- **~16%** — Average amount by which preliminary rates fall below 2026 Medicare rates
- **775** — Lab tests that would take the maximum 15% cut in 2027
- **$1B** — Estimated annual Medicare savings, per CMS

Of the **1,947 HCPCS codes** on the fee schedule, **1,528** had enough reported data to calculate a weighted median. Of those, **1,171 came in lower** than the 2026 Medicare rate, **186 came in higher**, and **169 were unchanged**. The rates were computed from **6,304 laboratories** after exclusions (6,411 submitted), using private-payer data collected January 1 through June 30, 2025 and reported May 1 through July 31, 2026.

### The 15% Cap Is What Keeps This From Being a Cliff

The realized cut in 2027 will be smaller than the raw data implies, because 2026 federal budget legislation capped CLFS reductions at **15% per year for 2027 through 2029**. That cap is doing a lot of work. Before it is applied, the category-level reductions look like this:

| Test category | Uncapped change vs. 2026 | What it means for 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| **Genomic sequencing** | −23% | Capped at −15% in 2027; remainder lands in 2028 |
| **Molecular pathology** | −22% | Capped at −15% in 2027; remainder lands in 2028 |
| **Microbiology** | −19.3% | Capped at −15% in 2027 |
| **Immunology** | −19.3% | Capped at −15% in 2027 |
| **Chemistry** | −16% | Capped at −15% in 2027 |
| **Proprietary lab analyses** | −2.4% | Passes through largely intact |

Read that table as a **three-year glide path, not a one-year event**. A code whose private-payer median sits 23% below its current Medicare rate takes 15% in 2027 and the balance in 2028. Any practice running an in-office lab — chemistry panels in primary care, urinalysis and cultures in urology, molecular testing in oncology, GI pathogen panels — should be modeling 2027 *and* 2028 now, because the 2028 number is already visible in this data.

**Comment Deadline:** CMS is accepting written comments on the preliminary rates for **30 days** — roughly through **October 21, 2026** — by email to **CLFS_Annual_Public_Meeting@cms.hhs.gov**. Final rates publish in **November**. If a code you bill at volume came in with a median that looks wrong for your market, this is the only window to say so with data.

### The Legislative Track

The bipartisan **RESULTS Act (H.R. 5269)**, cosponsored by **62 Republicans and 59 Democrats**, would replace the 15% annual cap with a **5% cap**. It has not moved. Until it does, budget against the 15% figure.

### Meanwhile, October 1 Arrives Without a Shutdown

One thing practices do *not* have to plan for next week: a Medicare claims hold. Congress cleared a continuing resolution on **September 1** — **370–48** in the House after a **90–6** Senate vote — and the President signed it on **September 2**, funding the government through **December 11, 2026**. Last year’s 43-day shutdown began on October 1 and briefly froze telehealth claims; this year, October 1 is a normal claims day. Medicare telehealth flexibilities, extended by the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026, run through **December 31, 2027**. The next funding cliff is **77 days** away.

## Section 02 — Prior Authorization / Payer Relations

### Aetna Folds Four Cancer Authorizations Into One, Cigna’s Algorithm Keeps Downcoding Level 4 and 5 Visits — and Humana’s 600,000 Non-Renewal Letters Are Dated October 2

Last week this newsletter covered UnitedHealthcare’s October 1 removal of prior authorization on roughly 1,700 codes. This week the payer story splits three ways: one insurer consolidating authorizations, one insurer automating payment reductions, and one insurer shrinking its Medicare Advantage footprint. All three change what your front desk and billing team do in the fourth quarter.

**Stat cards:**
- **4 → 1** — Average separate prior authorizations per Aetna member with cancer, now bundled into one
- **~25%** — Share of eligible members who used bundled cancer authorizations in Aetna’s pilot
- **600,000** — Humana Medicare Advantage members in plans exiting for 2027
- **1 in 8** — Standard prior authorization requests denied in government programs last year

### Aetna: One Authorization for the Whole Cancer Treatment Plan

Aetna is **bundling separate prior authorizations for cancer treatment** — chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiation oncology services — together with the associated imaging, including **MRI and CT**. Providers previously submitted an average of **four separate prior authorizations per Aetna member with cancer**. The pilot, launched last summer for **lung, breast, and prostate cancers**, saw nearly **25%** of eligible members use the bundle.

The bundle became available to **Medicaid members in eligible states on September 2**, with the fall rollout covering **Oklahoma, Illinois, Maryland, New Jersey, Virginia, Florida, Kentucky, and West Virginia**. Full rollout to **Medicare and commercial members** is planned for the **first half of 2027**. Aetna covers 26 million members. Separately, insurers participating in the HHS prior authorization pledge have cut **11% of preapprovals** since last summer.

**Key Insight:** A bundled authorization changes the unit of work, not just the count. An oncology authorization coordinator who used to work four requests per patient now works one larger, more clinically complete request — which means the documentation has to be assembled *up front* for the whole treatment plan. Practices that build the packet once, at the treatment-planning visit, will get the benefit. Practices that keep submitting piecemeal will not.

### Cigna: The Downcoding Policy Is Now a Year Old and Still Running

Cigna’s **E/M Coding Accuracy Policy (R49)**, in effect since **October 1, 2025**, allows the payer to **automatically reduce level 4 and 5 E/M claims by one level** when its claims algorithm decides the submitted diagnoses do not support the complexity billed. The codes in scope are **99204, 99205, 99214, 99215, 99244, and 99245**. On **September 3, 2026**, the American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery submitted formal opposition letters to Cigna challenging the policy. Several Blue Cross Blue Shield licensees have acknowledged similar recoding practices in specific situations.

The practical point: a downcoded claim is **paid**, not denied, so it rarely appears on a denial report. The only way to see it is **payment variance analysis** — comparing the CPT billed to the CPT adjudicated on the 835. If your denial dashboard is clean and your Cigna E/M revenue per visit is falling, this is why.

### Humana: 600,000 Members, Letters Dated October 2

Humana confirmed on its **July 29** earnings call that Medicare Advantage plan exits for 2027 will affect about **600,000 members** — roughly **8% of its 7.2 million** MA members — concentrated in plans rated **3.5 stars or lower**. Federally required non-renewal notices are dated **October 2**, and Medicare open enrollment runs **October 15 through December 7**. Humana recaptured just over **40%** of members affected by its 2025 exits and expects a similar share this time.

**Action Required:** Pull your active Humana MA patients by plan ID now and cross-reference against the exiting plans in your market. For every patient in an exiting plan, January 1 means a new payer, a new eligibility record, possibly a new prior authorization for an in-progress treatment course, and a new network status for your practice. Flag those accounts for **re-verification at the first January visit** — not at the first denial.

## Section 03 — AI & Automation Deep Dive

### Oracle Pushes AI Upstream Into Scheduling and Financial Clearance — While Only 19% of Practices Already Using AI Say It Has Actually Reduced Their Work

At its Health and Life Sciences Summit in Orlando on **September 23, 2026**, **Oracle Health** announced a set of AI capabilities spanning **scheduling, financial clearance, clinical documentation, charge capture, billing, and payment**. The framing was explicit: move AI *upstream*, to catch denials, missing information, authorization problems, and inaccurate charges before they become rework. The capabilities are planned for general availability “in the coming months.”

**Stat cards:**
- **19%** — Current AI users at physician practices reporting meaningful net work reduction (Black Book)
- **96%** — AI evaluators who require integration with existing systems
- **89%** — Who require defined performance metrics before expanding an AI deployment
- **5** — Named Oracle Health AI revenue cycle capabilities announced September 23

### What Oracle Actually Announced

| Capability | Cycle stage | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Autonomous prior authorization** | Front end | Evaluates payer criteria, retrieves documentation rules, pre-populates clinical fields, attaches chart evidence |
| **Clinical documentation integrity** | Mid-cycle | Analyzes notes in real time against reimbursement criteria and flags gaps |
| **Charge capture & integrity** | Mid-cycle | Reads procedural narratives and service codes to prevent revenue leakage |
| **Autonomous professional fee coding** | Mid-cycle | Suggests CPT, HCPCS, and ICD-10 codes inside the coder workflow |
| **Automated appeal management** | Back end | Evaluates remittance advice, isolates underpayments, compiles appeal packets |

No customer names, performance metrics, or dates were attached. That is not unusual for a conference announcement, but it matters for how you file it: this is a **roadmap**, not a result. Note also where the list starts — with prior authorization — and where it ends — with *underpayment* detection rather than denial appeals. That last detail lines up with the Cigna downcoding problem in Section 02: the revenue a practice loses to a paid-but-reduced claim is invisible to a denial-centric workflow.

### The Number That Should Anchor Every AI Conversation This Fall

Black Book’s September 2026 preconference flash survey of **1,184 physician-practice executives**, released **September 24** ahead of next week’s MGMA Annual Conference, found that **only 19% of current AI users report meaningful net work reduction**. Buyers have responded by raising the bar: **96%** of AI evaluators require integration with existing systems, **89%** require defined performance metrics before expanding, and **85%** require **measurable first-year productivity outcomes**. A companion Black Book survey of **1,919 clinicians** found **81%** require daily workarounds and **65%** lose **30 or more minutes a day** to avoidable digital work.

**Key Insight:** Black Book’s own summary puts it precisely: the conversation “has shifted from what a system can do to what work the organization can actually stop doing after implementation.” That is the right procurement question. Before you sign, write down the specific task, the FTE hours it consumes today, and who stops doing it on go-live day. If the vendor cannot help you fill in that last column, the 81% will stay at 81%.

### The Coding-Intensity Side Effect

Ambient documentation is the AI category that *has* produced measurable change — and payers have noticed what it changed. Across large health systems adopting ambient scribes, visits have shifted toward higher-intensity E/M codes; one system saw a **5% increase in Level 5 encounters**, worth more than **$1,000 per provider per month**. One 2026 study projected up to **$2.3 billion** in added healthcare costs industry-wide. Payers are answering with the automated downcoding described in Section 02. The operational lesson: if an ambient tool raises your level-of-service mix, your **documentation has to support the new level on audit**, because the payer’s algorithm will test it.

## Section 04 — Coding Updates

### Six Days, Four Code Files: ICD-10-CM, ICD-10-PCS, the October HCPCS Quarterly, and New NCCI Edits All Turn Over on Thursday, October 1

October 1, 2026 falls on a **Thursday**. That is the effective date for four separate code-file changes, each governed by **date of service**, not date of submission. Services on Wednesday, September 30 use the old files regardless of when they are billed; services on Thursday use the new ones.

**Stat cards:**
- **6** — Days until October 1, 2026 — a Thursday
- **4** — Code files changing: ICD-10-CM, ICD-10-PCS, HCPCS Level II quarterly, NCCI PTP edits
- **100+** — New FY 2027 ICD-10-PCS procedure codes
- **19** — New HCPCS codes added to the home health consolidated billing supply list

| File | Posted | Effective | Who it hits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FY 2027 ICD-10-CM** | Summer 2026 (with 2027 Conversion Table) | October 1, 2026 | Every practice |
| **FY 2027 ICD-10-PCS** (100+ new codes) | Summer 2026 | October 1, 2026 | Inpatient coders; surgeons whose hospital coding drives co-management |
| **October 2026 HCPCS Level II quarterly** | Alpha-numeric file updated September 10, 2026 | October 1, 2026 | Drugs, biologicals, supplies, DME |
| **NCCI PTP practitioner edits, Q4** | September 1, 2026 | October 1, 2026 | Every practice billing code pairs |
| **UHC MA Observation & Discharge policy** | UHC reimbursement bulletin | October 1, 2026 | Hospitalists; any practice billing observation care to UHC MA |

### The Three-Day Readiness Test

Last week’s issue walked through the ICD-10-CM deletions and demotions in detail (I42.0, Z68.1, the S23.42 series). This week the question is narrower: **is the new code set actually installed and working?** With six days left, there is time for exactly one round of testing.

- **Monday–Tuesday:** Confirm with your EHR and PM vendors, in writing, that the FY 2027 ICD-10-CM file and the October HCPCS file are loaded to production and will activate by date of service. Ask whether the NCCI Q4 edits are loaded into your claim scrubber — many practice scrubbers lag the CMS posting.
- **Wednesday:** Build test claims with an October 1 date of service using at least one new ICD-10-CM code and one deleted code. The new code should pass; the deleted code should be stopped by your scrubber, not by the payer.
- **Thursday onward:** Pull a daily rejection report by reason code for the first ten business days of October. Code-set rejections cluster in the first week; the practices that catch them early re-bill in days instead of weeks.

**Warning:** The October HCPCS quarterly is where **drug and biological codes** change mid-year. Any practice buying and billing drugs — oncology, rheumatology, ophthalmology, urology, allergy — should reconcile its chargemaster against the October file before the first infusion day in October. A J-code that changed on October 1 and still sits in your fee schedule under the old descriptor is a denial on your highest-dollar claim line.

### Downcoding Makes Your E/M Documentation a Coding Update, Too

Cigna’s R49 policy (Section 02) targets **99204, 99205, 99214, 99215, 99244, and 99245** based on the diagnoses on the claim. That turns **diagnosis specificity into an E/M payment variable**. A level 4 visit coded with a vague or unspecified diagnosis is the claim an algorithm downcodes; the same visit coded with the specific, supported comorbidities the physician actually addressed is the claim that holds. The October 1 ICD-10-CM update adds specificity in several areas — use it.

## Section 05 — Revenue Velocity / KPIs

### Patients Owe More and Pay Less: HCA Flags a Self-Pay Collections Slowdown as Members Slide From Silver to Bronze — and 32% of Groups See No-Shows Rising

Speaking at the **Jefferies Healthcare Services and Technology Conference** in Nashville on **September 15**, HCA Healthcare CFO **Mike Marks** described a pattern every practice billing office will recognize: patients are **owing more** because of benefit design across employer plans, Medicare Advantage, and the exchanges — including movement **from silver to bronze plans** — but they are **not paying more**. HCA has seen a **slowdown in its ability to collect out-of-pocket amounts**, which it attributes to energy costs, premiums, and inflation.

**Stat cards:**
- **32%** — Medical groups reporting higher no-show rates year to date vs. 2025 (MGMA Stat)
- **91%** — Practice executives naming denials and payment delays a top 2027 improvement priority
- **94%** — Practices reporting rising expenses without comparable reimbursement growth
- **37%** — Prioritizing patient access and scheduling technology for 2027

When the largest hospital operator in the country says its self-pay collections are slowing, a physician practice with a fraction of HCA’s patient financial services capacity should assume the same is happening to it — and that it is happening faster. An **August 11 MGMA Stat poll** found **32%** of medical groups reporting higher no-show rates year to date, **58%** about the same, and only **10%** lower. Higher patient cost-sharing shows up first as a missed appointment and second as an unpaid balance.

### The Bronze-Plan Problem Is a Timing Problem

A patient who moved from a silver to a bronze exchange plan for 2026 has a larger deductible, and the deductible resets January 1. That makes **October through December the months when bronze-plan patients have finally met, or nearly met, their deductibles** — and January through March the months when they owe the most per visit. Two practical consequences:

- **Q4 is the window for deferred care.** Patients who have met their deductible are the ones most likely to schedule the procedure they postponed. Outreach to those patients before December 31 is revenue that disappears on January 1.
- **Q1 needs estimates at scheduling, not at check-in.** The January patient with a reset deductible needs to hear the number before the visit, with a payment plan option, or the visit becomes a no-show or a bad-debt balance.

### Where You Should Be — Patient Financial KPIs

| Metric | Warning zone | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Days in A/R | > 45 | < 35 |
| Patient A/R share of total A/R | > 30% | < 20% |
| Point-of-service collection rate (on known patient responsibility) | < 50% | ≥ 70% |
| Estimates delivered before the visit | < 50% of scheduled visits | ≥ 90% |
| Card on file / payment plan at estimate | Ad hoc | Standard workflow |
| No-show rate | > 10% | < 5% |
| First-pass denial rate | > 10% | < 5% |
| Patient bad debt (% of net patient revenue) | > 5% | < 3% |

**Action Required:** Automate the reminder, the estimate, and the payment link as one message, sent 72 hours before the visit. Each of the three on its own is a known workflow; combined, they convert a no-show risk into a scheduled payment. It is the cheapest revenue velocity project available to a practice this quarter.

## Section 07 — Compliance Corner

### OIG Finds Roughly Four in Five Sampled Medicare Advantage Diagnoses Unsupported — and the Documentation Standard Behind That Finding Lives in Your Charts

On **September 17, 2026**, the HHS Office of Inspector General published compliance audits finding that Medicare Advantage plans operated by **Humana** and **UnitedHealthcare** received an estimated **$177.8 million** in overpayments for **2020 and 2021** after submitting diagnosis codes not supported by patients’ medical records.

**Stat cards:**
- **$177.8M** — Estimated MA overpayments at HumanaChoice and UnitedHealthcare of Wisconsin, 2020–2021
- **178 / 220** — HumanaChoice sampled enrollee-years not supported by medical records
- **183 / 250** — UnitedHealthcare of Wisconsin sampled enrollee-years unsupported
- **11** — High-risk diagnosis groups OIG examined

| Plan | Unsupported sample | Extrapolated overpayment |
|---|---|---|
| **HumanaChoice (H5216)** | 178 of 220 (81%) | ~$130.9 million |
| **UnitedHealthcare of Wisconsin** | 183 of 250 (73%) | ~$47 million |

OIG examined **11 groups of high-risk diagnosis codes**, including **acute stroke, heart attack, embolism, and sepsis** — the conditions most often coded from a historical problem list rather than from a current, treated encounter. Both insurers said the methodology was skewed toward finding overpayments and that they do not plan to refund. Earlier in 2026, OIG issued similar audits of Blue Cross Blue Shield of Alabama, Gateway Health Plan, and Priority Health.

### Why a Payer Audit Is a Practice Compliance Issue

Medicare Advantage risk-adjustment diagnoses are **drawn from provider encounter records** — the physician claim and the note behind it. The plan bears the refund; the practice bears the consequences that follow: more chart retrieval requests, more retrospective risk-adjustment reviews, and more payer scrutiny of diagnoses that appear on a claim without a matching assessment and plan in the note. The documentation standard is the same one CMS applies in RADV audits: a diagnosis counts if the record shows it was **monitored, evaluated, assessed, or treated** at that encounter.

**Warning:** The four named conditions are exactly the ones that tend to be mis-carried in ambulatory records: “history of stroke” coded as *acute* stroke, a months-old MI still coded as acute, a resolved embolism still on the problem list. Run a query this week for acute stroke, acute MI, and embolism codes on office-visit claims. Every hit without a same-encounter assessment and plan is a finding waiting to happen — and, with the ambient-scribe coding intensity trend in Section 03, one more reason payers are pulling charts.

## Section 08 — Independent Practice Watch

### 94% Say Costs Are Outrunning Reimbursement, 72% Can’t Keep Revenue Cycle Staff — and 1% of Physician Office Labs Helped Set Next Year’s Lab Rates

Black Book’s September flash survey is a practice-side survey, and its operational findings read like a portrait of the independent group:

**Stat cards:**
- **94%** — Rising expenses without comparable reimbursement growth
- **89%** — Name payer administrative requirements a significant burden
- **72%** — Struggle to recruit or retain revenue cycle personnel
- **58%** — Struggle with disconnected systems and duplicate data entry

Add **82%** citing staffing shortages or labor costs and **79%** reporting increased concern about cybersecurity risk. Only **16%** are considering outsourcing administrative functions — which means the other 84% intend to solve a staffing problem with the staff they have, plus software.

### How This Week’s News Lands on a Small Practice

**The lab fee schedule was set without you.** Only about **1% of physician office labs** reported private-payer data in this cycle, compared with roughly **2%** of all Medicare Part B labs and fewer than **20%** of hospital outreach labs; hospital outreach labs made up **13.6%** of all reporting labs. The rates your in-office lab will be paid in 2027 were derived largely from independent and hospital labs with very different cost structures and payer contracts. A small practice running CBCs, metabolic panels, and urinalysis in-house will absorb up to a 15% cut on rates it had essentially no role in setting. Run the in-office lab P&L against the capped 2027 rates and the 2028 remainder before renewing analyzer leases or reagent contracts.

**Downcoding hits hardest where there is no one to catch it.** A health system has a payer-contracting team running payment variance reports. A four-physician practice has a biller who posts the 835 and moves on. Cigna’s R49 reductions are paid claims — they do not generate a denial for anyone to work. The small practice is the one least equipped to notice.

**Humana’s MA exits cost a small practice proportionally more.** A practice with a concentrated Medicare panel in a market where Humana is exiting can see a meaningful share of its patients change payers on January 1. Each one is a re-verification, a new card, and potentially a new authorization for an ongoing treatment course.

**The retention finding is the one to act on.** When **72%** of practices can’t keep revenue cycle staff, every workflow that depends on one experienced biller’s memory is a single point of failure. Documenting payer-specific rules — UHC’s code-and-plan PA list, Aetna’s oncology bundle, Cigna’s R49 codes — in a shared payer matrix is the lowest-cost retention insurance available.

Ten moves for the week of September 25, 2026 — each tied directly to something in this issue.
