Two conversion factors, both down, in the second year of the statutory split between APM and non-APM participants. A practice that has not yet decided whether an APM pathway is worth the administrative overhead is now looking at a 33-cent per-RVU spread — small in isolation, meaningful at volume, and permanent in direction.

The Proposal Almost Nobody Commented On

The conversion factor is the number that gets the press release. The proposal that will change more practice economics over the next five years is the practice expense methodology rewrite, and it has drawn a fraction of the attention.

CMS proposes to phase out specialty-specific practice-expense-per-hour data anchored to surveys from 2007 or earlier. That data is the foundation of the indirect practice expense allocation for a large share of specialties — it is, in effect, a twenty-year-old snapshot of what it costs to run an office, still being used to price what it costs to run an office in 2027. Replacing it is defensible. Replacing it reallocates, because practice expense RVUs are budget-neutral: every specialty that gains does so at the expense of one that loses, and the specialties still priced on the oldest survey data are the ones whose numbers move most.

Two related proposals sit alongside it:

  • Indirect PE allocation for skilled nursing facility visits would be adjusted, affecting any practice with a meaningful SNF rounding book.
  • CMS is soliciting comment on whether the facility versus non-facility payment differential remains appropriate. That is a Request for Information, not a proposal — which is exactly why it matters. RFIs in the PFS are how CMS tests a policy one year before proposing it. The site-of-service differential is the single largest structural subsidy in office-based practice economics, and CMS has now put it in writing that it is looking at it.

The Smaller Changes That Hit Billing on January 1

Four proposals will land directly in front-line billing workflow if finalized:

  1. Remote therapy monitoring gets three new restrictions. Payment would be limited to established patients only, would require a separately reportable initiating visit, and would be payable only when performed by employed clinical staff — not contractors. That last clause is the one to read twice. A substantial share of RTM and remote-monitoring programs at independent practices are delivered through contracted monitoring vendors under a staffing arrangement. If the employment requirement is finalized as proposed, those arrangements stop being payable on January 1, 2027, and the fix is contractual, not clinical.
  2. Shared medical appointments would get separate coding and payment for the first time.
  3. Advance care planning would be split: two new HCPCS codes for clinical-staff-delivered services, with existing CPT codes 99497 and 99498 limited to practitioner-delivered services.
  4. Smoking and tobacco cessation and SBIRT join the timed behavioral health code upward adjustment in its final transition year.
Warning

The remote therapy monitoring proposal would pay only for services performed by employed clinical staff, not contractors. If your RTM or remote monitoring program runs through a vendor staffing arrangement — and many independent practice programs do — it stops being payable on January 1, 2027 as proposed. This is a contract problem with a three-day comment window and a 112-day remediation window.

One Clock That Already Stopped, in a Good Way

Amid the deadlines, one item is settled. The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026, signed February 3 after the 43-day partial government shutdown, extended Medicare telehealth flexibilities and the work GPCI floor through December 31, 2027. CMS instructed that claims be paid as they had been before October 1, 2025, and that the flexibilities apply retroactively across the lapse period.

That retroactivity has an unclaimed-revenue tail that most practices have not worked. CMS explicitly asked practitioners to identify beneficiaries who paid out of pocket for telehealth furnished during the lapse, refund those payments, and submit the claims to Medicare instead. For a practice that ran cash-pay telehealth during the shutdown window, that is a defined, finite list of encounters that can still be converted to Medicare revenue.

Track One: Gold Carding Is Getting a Longer Memory

Texas — the state that invented provider gold carding with HB 3459 in 2021 — strengthened its law through HB 3812, effective September 1, 2026. The change is technical and consequential: exemption status is now evaluated against a full year of prior authorization requests rather than six months.

Read that from the practice side. A longer evaluation window means a larger denominator, which means one bad quarter no longer disqualifies a practice from exemption — and equally, that a practice cannot earn exemption on a favorable six-month stretch. Gold carding just became a sustained-performance program instead of a streak. As of 2026, five states have enacted gold carding: Texas, Louisiana, Michigan, Vermont, and West Virginia.

Medicare is testing the same idea. CMMI signalled it intends to pilot a gold-carding feature within the WISeR model by mid-2026, exempting clinicians with consistent approval histories from future prior authorization or pre-payment review. WISeR is running in six states — New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas, Arizona, and Washington — with coverage determinations due within 72 hours, or 48 for expedited cases, and with contractors explicitly not incentivized to deny.

Track Two: Denials Are Going the Other Way

Prior authorization denials are up 31% year over year across commercial and Medicare Advantage payers. That is the number that makes the gold-carding expansion feel smaller than it sounds: an exemption program covering a subset of services for a subset of providers in five states does not offset a nationwide 31% increase in the base rate.

Track Three: Providers Are Exercising the Only Leverage They Have

Twenty-nine health systems are dropping Medicare Advantage plans in 2026, and the reasons given are consistent across all of them: prior authorization friction, claim denials, and slow or low reimbursement.

The published count almost certainly understates the trend — smaller and less-publicized non-renewals never make the list. And MA now covers more than half of eligible Medicare beneficiaries, which means each exit is a larger share of a practice’s panel than it would have been five years ago.

The Federal Clock: 112 Days

Under CMS-0057-F, impacted payers — Medicare Advantage organizations, Medicaid and CHIP managed care entities, state Medicaid agencies, and QHP issuers on the federally facilitated exchanges — must stand up four FHIR APIs by January 1, 2027: Patient Access, Provider Access, Payer-to-Payer, and Prior Authorization.

The decision-timeframe half of that rule has already been in force since January 1, 2026: 72 hours expedited, 7 days standard, with specific denial reasons required. The first public prior authorization metrics reports, covering calendar year 2025, were due March 31, 2026. Separately, Cigna has committed to standardizing more than 70% of its prior authorization volume for medical services by the end of 2026, part of the industry-wide commitment UnitedHealthcare, Aetna, Elevance and Cigna signed onto with a framework target of January 1, 2027.

Action Required

Build your gold-card dossier now, whether or not your state has a program. Pull twelve months of prior authorization requests by payer and by service line, and calculate your approval rate for each. Texas has just made twelve months the standard of proof, WISeR is piloting the same concept in Medicare, and every payer running a voluntary exemption program asks for the same data. A practice that cannot produce that table on request is not eligible for any of it — and the table is also the single best input to a contract negotiation.

The Foundation-Model Turn: Maverick Ships Clinical-to-Revenue, Xsolis Automates the Peer-to-Peer, Abridge Wires NEJM and JAMA Into the Note — While 62% Say Fragmented Data Is What Actually Blocks Scale

Three product announcements landed in the last week, and read together they describe a single shift: RCM AI is moving from point tools that do one task to models that hold the whole clinical-to-cash path in one representation.

Maverick Medical AI released its Clinical-to-Revenue Foundation Model on September 8, 2026, positioning it as a single intelligence layer that begins at the point of care and runs through autonomous, billing-ready coding — spanning physician documentation, coding, compliance, medical necessity, and denial prevention. The framing the company chose is “revenue certainty,” which is a deliberate step away from the accuracy-percentage marketing that has defined autonomous coding for three years.

Xsolis announced general availability of its GenAI Peer-to-Peer Clinical Synopsis. This one deserves more attention than it will get. The peer-to-peer review is the last fully manual, physician-time-consuming step in the denial workflow — a scheduled phone call in which a treating physician argues medical necessity to a payer medical director, prepared for by hand, at a cost measured in clinical hours rather than staff hours. Automating the synopsis a physician walks into that call with is a narrow intervention with an unusually favorable ratio: it saves the most expensive minutes in the revenue cycle. In KLAS survey data, 89% of Xsolis users say they rely on its AI to minimize preventable denials.

Abridge announced a partnership with NEJM and JAMA to surface evidence-based guidance inside ambient clinical conversations, explicitly to ease downstream coding burden. That is documentation-side AI acknowledging that its output is a revenue cycle input.

The Provider–Payer Gap Is Still Two to One

The 2025 CAQH Index, released February 19, 2026, found that more than 50% of health plans use AI tools in administrative workflows, against 25% of provider organizations. Payers are automating the adjudication side at twice the rate providers are automating the submission side — and the party with the faster automation writes the rules of the exchange.

The narrowest and best-documented gap remains denials. Only about 14% of providers use AI specifically to reduce denials, yet 69% of those who do report fewer denials and better resubmissions. It is the highest-yield, least-adopted application in the revenue cycle, for the third consecutive quarter of survey data.

The three product announcements this week all attack the same target — the handoff. January 1, 2027: The Change That Touches Contracts, Not Just Code Books

CPT 2027 restructures maternity care services for the first time in roughly three decades. Maternity care becomes reportable at the service level across four separately identified phases — antepartum, labor management, delivery, and postpartum — and the familiar bundled global maternity codes are deleted.

For an OB practice this is not a coding update. It is a contracting event. Commercial obstetric contracts are overwhelmingly priced against the global codes: a single negotiated rate for a global obstetric package. When the codes those rates are attached to cease to exist on January 1, every one of those contract line items needs a replacement rate structure — negotiated, loaded, and tested — and the party that arrives at that negotiation without a modeled expectation of the four-phase total will accept whatever the payer’s crosswalk produces.

Two further CPT 2027 items:

  • 92507 is deleted effective January 1, 2027, replaced by new speech-language pathology codes. Any practice employing SLPs — pediatrics, ENT, rehab, neurology — is rebuilding its most frequently billed therapy code.
  • The AMA’s biannual Category III update adds 58 new Category III codes and revises three descriptors, effective January 1, 2027 after the mandatory six-month implementation period. Notable entries: 1072T–1078T for services related to liver allografts prepared on a hypothermic oxygenated perfusion device; 1054T for focused non-shock-wave ultrasonic propulsion of residual stone fragments after lithotripsy; 1056T for cochlear infusion of a pharmacologic agent; and 1069T–1071T for machine-based cryocompression addressing chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy.

The Edit That Arrives Before Any of Them

While the code sets turn over on federal schedules, payer policy turns over on the payer’s schedule — and one lands Wednesday. Effective September 16, 2026, EviCore by Evernorth updates the esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) guidelines supporting prior authorization and medical necessity determinations for Horizon Blue Cross Blue Shield of New Jersey’s Gastroenterology Program.

That is the recurring lesson of every October. The claim that fails is rarely the one carrying an unfamiliar new diagnosis code — coders are trained and vigilant on new codes. It is the routine, correctly coded claim that hits a payer edit nobody read, because payer policy updates do not come with a CMS press release and a 90-day runway.

Warning

If you bill obstetrics, start the contract work now, not in December. The global maternity codes are deleted on January 1, 2027, and every commercial contract priced against them needs a four-phase replacement structure. Model your own antepartum-plus-labor-plus-delivery-plus-postpartum total before you see the payer’s proposed crosswalk. Practices that walk into that conversation without a number will be handed one.

$258 Billion Avoided, $21 Billion Left on the Table — and the Manual Eligibility Check Still Costs 24 Minutes and $14

The 2025 CAQH Index, released February 19, 2026, is the closest thing the industry has to an audited scoreboard on administrative automation. Its headline finding is genuinely good news: U.S. healthcare avoided $258 billion in administrative spending in 2024 by conducting transactions electronically — a 17% increase in cost avoidance year over year, achieved despite the continuing fallout of the 2024 Change Healthcare cyberattack. Medical administrative spend fell 9%; dental fell 4%.

The second finding is the one to budget against: $21 billion in savings remains available from fully automating the transactions still handled manually or partially manually.

What a Manual Transaction Still Costs

The aggregate numbers are abstract. The per-transaction numbers are not:

  • A manual eligibility check by phone takes about 24 minutes and costs roughly $14 in staff time. At 50 verifications a day, that is approximately $700 per day — and it is the transaction with the largest single savings opportunity in the Index.
  • A manual prior authorization costs a provider $10.97, against $5.79 fully electronic. On the payer side the same transaction runs $3.41 manual versus $0.05 electronic — a ratio that explains, with no further commentary needed, why payer-side automation is running at twice the provider rate.
  • Fully automated administrative workflows save an average of 70 minutes per patient visit across participants.

Denials: The Overturn Rate Is the Underrated Metric

Denial benchmarks have settled into an uncomfortable band. The average initial denial rate is approximately 11.8% across payer types, with some categories above 19%; hospital denial rates approach 10% broadly, and the marketplace-plan average has reached nearly 20%. Unresolved denials alone can represent up to $5 million in annual revenue loss for a single organization.

Against that, one worked example is worth more than any benchmark table. Cleveland Clinic runs a 15% incoming denial rate — worse than the industry average. In 2025 its revenue cycle team overturned 92% of those denials, bringing net controllable loss to under 2%.

The lesson for a ten-physician practice is not that it should hire Cleveland Clinic’s denial team. It is that the incoming denial rate and the net loss rate are different numbers, and most practices only track the first. A 15% denial rate with a 92% overturn is a healthier revenue cycle than an 8% denial rate with a 40% overturn — and the second practice will report the better-looking KPI to its board.

The Patient Balance Is Now the Slow Half of the Cycle

  • Over 70% of providers report that collecting patient balances takes a month or more.
  • Roughly 25% of payments to small providers arrive after the due date.
  • Nearly 77% of patient out-of-pocket dollars now sit in hard-to-collect categories: uninsured, underinsured, digitally disengaged, or facing large and complex bills.
  • Practices deploying patient-led check-in kiosks report a 154% increase in time-of-service collections, on a 96% patient adoption rate.

The kiosk figure is the actionable one, because time-of-service collection is the only patient-balance intervention with no aging curve attached. Every dollar not collected at the desk enters a 30-, 60-, or 90-day pipeline that costs money to run and loses value the whole way down.

The Buy-Versus-Build Question Has Already Been Answered

97% of healthcare organizations now outsource at least one RCM function, and 70% plan to expand outsourcing in the coming year. The pricing is well established: 4% to 9% of net collections on a percentage model, or $4 to $12 per claim on a per-claim model.

The reason is not primarily cost. It is labor. 63% of providers report staffing gaps in their RCM departments; independent tallies put roughly two in three organizations short-staffed in revenue cycle. Billing department turnover runs above 22% annually. Certified coders start at $55,000–$75,000 in major markets, with experienced CPC-credentialed staff frequently above $90,000.

Run the arithmetic a five-physician practice actually faces. One certified coder at $75,000 plus benefits is roughly $95,000 fully loaded, against a 22% annual chance of having to re-recruit and re-train the role. At a 6% outsourcing rate, that same $95,000 buys coverage on approximately $1.6 million of net collections — with no turnover exposure and no single-point-of-failure risk when one person takes vacation in October.

That is not an argument for outsourcing. It is an argument for pricing your in-house function honestly, including turnover cost, before comparing.

Procode AI launched an AI-powered RCM offering for surgical billing, backed by new funding and an acquisition — the now-familiar pattern of acquiring an established billing business and embedding AI into its existing workflows rather than selling software into a cold market. Meanwhile, venture funding for AI-powered RCM continues to surge, and private equity and strategic acquirers are concentrating capital on companies with AI-driven automation and scalable technology.

Compliance Corner: Price Transparency Enforcement Turns Six Months Old, CMS Asks How to Rewrite the Machine-Readable File, and Four FHIR APIs Come Due in 112 Days

Hospital price transparency enforcement of the updated requirements began April 1, 2026 — six months ago as of this issue. The revised rules, finalized in the CY 2026 OPPS/ASC final rule, changed the data elements hospitals must include in publicly available machine-readable files, with the stated aim of ensuring hospitals provide meaningful, accurate information about what they actually charge.

CMS is not finished. It has issued a Request for Information seeking feedback on strengthening MRF requirements, specifically on:

  • Increasing standardization of hospital pricing data
  • Improving accuracy and completeness of reporting within free-text fields
  • Enhancing the comparability and usefulness of the consumer-friendly display requirement

The free-text field item is the tell. Free text is where non-compliance hides in a technically compliant file — a machine-readable file that is readable by machines but not comparable across hospitals satisfies the letter of the rule and defeats its purpose. Congressional pressure is running in the same direction, with Senators Braun and Hassan having formally called on CMS to enforce the rule. CMS publishes its enforcement activities and outcomes as a public dataset on data.cms.gov — which means any practice, competitor, or plaintiff’s attorney can see enforcement history without filing a request.

The Interoperability Deadline Is a Payer Mandate With a Provider Dependency

CMS-0057-F requires four FHIR APIs by January 1, 2027 — 112 days from today. The regulated parties are payers: Medicare Advantage organizations, Medicaid and CHIP managed care entities, state Medicaid agencies, and QHP issuers on the federally facilitated exchanges. No physician practice is a regulated entity under this rule.

That is precisely why practices are underprepared for it. The Provider Access API and the Prior Authorization API are built to be consumed by provider systems — the PA API is specifically designed to let a provider’s system query a payer’s prior authorization requirements and submit structured requests electronically instead of through a portal or a fax.

The compliance obligation belongs to the payer. The benefit only materializes if your practice management system or EHR can talk to the API. A practice whose vendor has no published roadmap for FHIR PA submission will spend 2027 watching a fax-elimination mandate go into effect while continuing to send faxes.

There is one question worth asking your EHR vendor in writing this month: what is your delivery date for CMS-0057-F Prior Authorization API and Provider Access API consumption, and is it included in our current subscription or priced separately? The answer to the second half is the one that will surprise people.

The long-run version of the same problem: operating cost per FTE physician rose more than 63% from 2013 to 2022, while the Medicare conversion factor rose 1.7%. And in CY 2027, as proposed, the conversion factor goes down again — to $33.17 or $32.84 depending on APM status.

There is no efficiency program that closes a 61-point spread. What there is, instead, is a set of choices about which costs a practice is willing to stop carrying manually.

Regulatory Burden Falls Hardest on the Smallest Denominator

60% of respondents to MGMA’s 2026 Regulatory Burden Report are in independent practices, and the report ties rising regulatory burden and falling reimbursement directly to physician burnout, consolidation, and reduced patient access.

The structural asymmetry is simple arithmetic. Every item in this issue — the September 14 comment deadline, the FY 2027 code turnover, the CPT 2027 maternity restructure, the CMS-0057-F API dependency, the Texas gold-carding recalculation, the EviCore EGD guideline update — costs approximately the same number of staff hours to absorb whether a practice has 4 physicians or 400. A health system amortizes that across a compliance department. A four-physician group amortizes it across the practice manager’s evenings.

How This Week’s News Lands Differently on a Small Practice

  • Gold carding requires data most small practices cannot produce. Texas HB 3812 now evaluates a full year of prior authorization requests. A practice without a system that logs PA submissions, outcomes, and turnaround by payer cannot demonstrate eligibility for an exemption it may already qualify for.
  • The Medicare Advantage exit is not available to you. Twenty-nine health systems are dropping MA contracts as a negotiating instrument. A four-physician group that terminates an MA contract covering 18% of its panel does not get a renegotiation; it gets an 18% revenue hole and a patient-access problem.
  • The practice expense rewrite is aimed squarely at office-based practice. Phasing out 2007-era PE/hour data and questioning the facility versus non-facility differential are proposals about your cost structure, decided in a comment file that closes Monday.
  • The FHIR API dependency is a vendor-leverage problem. A large system tells its EHR vendor what to build. An independent practice waits to find out.
  • 97% of organizations already outsource something. For an independent practice this is less a trend than a permission slip: the question is no longer whether to outsource a revenue cycle function, but which one, and at what honest comparison to in-house cost including 22% turnover.

Primary Care

Primary care is the specialty with the most at stake in Monday’s comment deadline and the least obvious hook to comment about. Its exposure is not a single code — it is the practice expense methodology. Phasing out specialty-specific PE/hour data anchored to 2007 or earlier, adjusting indirect PE allocation, and formally asking whether the facility versus non-facility differential is still appropriate are three proposals that jointly reprice the economics of seeing a patient in an office. On the constructive side, CMS proposes separate coding and payment for shared medical appointments and two new HCPCS codes for clinical-staff advance care planning, with 99497 and 99498 restricted to practitioner-delivered services — a small but genuine expansion of what a primary care team can bill for work it already performs.

Cardiology

The Ambulatory Specialty Model is now 112 days out. It is mandatory, two-sided risk, with no opt-out and no hardship exemption, running five performance years from January 1, 2027 through 2031 with payment years 2029–2033. CMS projects approximately 8,600 physicians in selected geographies — roughly a quarter of core-based statistical areas and metropolitan divisions — managing about 600,000 heart failure and low back pain episodes. Participants are scored on quality, cost, improvement activities, and interoperability and are exempt from MIPS reporting for the duration. On the coding side, October 1 brings dilated cardiomyopathy subdivision and added specificity for rare cardiac conditions — documentation changes that cardiology notes are not currently written to support.

Orthopedics

Orthopedic surgery carries the largest projected decrease in the CY 2027 proposed rule at −7%, concentrated on specialties that bill office visits alongside same-day procedures and on operating-room specialties whose flagship codes were revalued. This follows a CY 2026 final rule that applied a 2.5% efficiency adjustment to surgical procedures, orthopedic services, diagnostic imaging, and outpatient interventions, producing roughly −5% overall impacts for orthopedics that year. Orthopedics is also drafted into the ASM low back pain arm, which covers orthopedic surgery, neurosurgery, interventional pain, pain management, and PM&R. Two consecutive years of structural cuts plus mandatory risk is the most adverse combination facing any specialty in this issue.

Oncology

The most under-reported RCM story of the quarter belongs to radiation oncology, and it is a payer-behavior story rather than a coding story. Effective January 1, 2026, CMS consolidated external beam treatment delivery into three complexity tiers — 77402 (Level 1), 77407 (Level 2), 77412 (Level 3) — the largest radiation oncology overhaul in more than a decade, with the technical component of image guidance now bundled into the delivery codes rather than separately billable.

The codes are working as designed. The payers are not processing them as designed. Practices report that cases involving high-complexity care are being reclassified by insurers into lower-tier codes, and that roughly 50% of high-complexity claims are being flagged for additional review or denied. The gap between CMS’s projections and practice experience is not coming from the code structure; it is coming from adjudication. For a radiation oncology practice, the operational response is documentation-specific: the tier determinants — multiple isocenters, active motion management — must be explicit in the treatment record on every Level 3 claim, because a downgrade appealed without that documentation will not be overturned. Medical oncology, separately, gains secondary malignant neoplasm codes identifying specific metastatic sites on October 1, which is a medical-necessity defense asset.

Radiology

Two pressures, one immediate and one structural. Immediately: Aetna’s 15% payment reduction on radiology services billed with modifier CT that do not meet “Smart Dose” standards compliance has been live since September 1 and applies claim by claim. It is an equipment-attestation and dose-registry problem wearing a billing problem’s clothing, and it is not appealable on clinical grounds. Structurally, diagnostic imaging was named in the CY 2026 efficiency adjustment and remains exposed to the CY 2027 practice expense rewrite, where imaging’s high indirect-cost allocation makes it sensitive to any change in PE methodology.

Gastroenterology

The nearest deadline in this section is Wednesday, September 16, 2026, when EviCore by Evernorth updates the esophagogastroduodenoscopy guidelines underpinning prior authorization and medical necessity determinations for Horizon Blue Cross Blue Shield of New Jersey’s Gastroenterology Program. GI practices outside New Jersey should still read it: EviCore guideline revisions propagate across the plans that contract with EviCore, and an EGD criteria change is a high-volume event for any GI practice. More broadly, GI billing complexity in 2026 is being driven by prior authorization and medical necessity review rather than by code changes.

Behavioral Health and Speech-Language Pathology

The stability news holds: Medicare telehealth flexibilities are extended through December 31, 2027, which removes the recurring cliff that has disrupted behavioral health scheduling for three years. For CY 2027, CMS proposes adding smoking and tobacco cessation and SBIRT to the timed behavioral health code upward adjustment in its final transition year — the last opportunity for that adjustment to be extended to additional services. Speech-language pathology faces a harder change: 92507 is deleted January 1, 2027, replaced by new codes. It is the most frequently reported SLP service in most practices, which means fee schedules, superbills, authorization templates, and payer contracts all reference a code that will not exist.

OB / Maternal-Fetal Medicine

CPT 2027 deletes the bundled global maternity codes and replaces them with service-level reporting across antepartum, labor management, delivery, and postpartum, effective January 1, 2027 — the first restructure in roughly three decades. The clinical rationale is sound: global codes have long mispriced fragmented prenatal care. The revenue cycle consequence is that every commercial obstetric contract priced against a global code needs a new rate structure in 112 days, and OB practices that have not opened that conversation with their top three payers are already behind the payers’ own crosswalk modeling.

This Week’s Action Items

Ten moves for the week of September 11, 2026 — each tied directly to something in this issue.

  • File your CY 2027 PFS comment before Monday — three days. CMS-1848-P closes September 14. The highest-leverage comment this cycle is not about the conversion factor, which is statutory arithmetic. It is about the practice expense methodology rewrite and the facility versus non-facility differential RFI — two proposals that reprice office-based practice and have drawn far less comment volume than they warrant.
  • Audit every remote therapy monitoring arrangement for the contractor clause. CMS proposes paying for RTM only when performed by employed clinical staff, not contractors, plus established patients only and a separately reportable initiating visit. If your monitoring is delivered under a vendor staffing arrangement, that program stops being payable on January 1, 2027 unless the contract structure changes.
  • Work the telehealth retroactivity list. CMS instructed practitioners to identify beneficiaries who paid out of pocket for telehealth during the shutdown lapse, refund them, and bill Medicare instead. It is a finite, defined set of encounters and it is still convertible revenue.
  • Build the twelve-month prior authorization dossier. Texas HB 3812, effective September 1, now evaluates gold-carding eligibility on a full year of PA requests; WISeR is piloting the same concept in Medicare. Approval rate by payer, by service line, twelve months back — required for every exemption program and the best single input to your next contract negotiation.
  • Ask your EHR vendor, in writing, for a CMS-0057-F delivery date and price. Four FHIR APIs come due for payers on January 1, 2027 — 112 days. Your practice has no obligation and therefore no deadline, which is why this gets missed. If your system cannot consume the Prior Authorization API, the fax-elimination mandate happens around you.
  • Add denial overturn rate and net controllable loss to your monthly KPI package. Cleveland Clinic runs a 15% incoming denial rate and a 92% overturn, producing under 2% net controllable loss. Both metrics are computable from data you already have, and they will change which denials you decide to work.
  • Kill the routine phone eligibility check this quarter. A manual eligibility verification runs 24 minutes and about $14 — roughly $700 a day at 50 checks. Eligibility is the single largest remaining savings opportunity in the CAQH Index. Target ≥ 95% electronic with zero phone checks for routine plans.
  • If you bill obstetrics, open the maternity contract conversation with your top three payers now. The global codes are deleted January 1, 2027. Model your own four-phase total before the payer’s crosswalk arrives, or you will be negotiating against their number instead of yours.
  • If you bill radiation oncology, tighten Level 3 documentation before the next claim batch. Roughly 50% of high-complexity claims are being flagged or downgraded. Multiple isocenters and active motion management must be explicit in the treatment record — a tier downgrade appealed without that documentation does not get overturned.
  • Price your in-house revenue cycle honestly, including turnover. 97% of organizations outsource at least one function, 63% report RCM staffing gaps, and billing turnover runs above 22% annually. Outsourcing runs 4–9% of net collections or $4–12 per claim. You may well keep the function in house — but make that decision against a fully loaded number that includes the cost of refilling the role.
