## Section 01 — Regulatory / Payment Updates

### CMS Finalizes Transparency in Coverage Overhaul for Payers: Quarterly Reporting Replaces Monthly, Executives Must Vouch for Data Accuracy — and the 2027 Fee Schedule Final Rule Is Due in Six Weeks

Two regulatory events define the billing environment heading into year-end. On **October 5, 2026**, CMS, the Department of Labor, and the Treasury Department jointly finalized the **Transparency in Coverage (TiC) Final Rule (CMS 9882-F)** — the most significant revision to machine-readable pricing rules since they took effect in July 2022. The rule takes effect **December 5, 2026**, with data requirements phasing in through 2027. And separately, the **CY 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule final rule** is on track for release in **mid-November**, locking in the conversion factor and hundreds of coding policy decisions that practices have been modeling against the July proposal.

**Stat cards:**
- **December 5, 2026** — Effective date of the new Transparency in Coverage Final Rule
- **Quarterly** — New reporting frequency for in-network rate files (replacing monthly)
- **1.68%** — Proposed conversion factor cut for non-qualifying APM participants in 2027
- **54 days** — Until government funding expires (December 11, 2026)

### What the TiC Final Rule Changes for Billing Teams

The TiC rule primarily governs what **payers** must publish, but its downstream effects land on practices: when the rate file a payer posts is wrong or outdated, referral decisions, out-of-network disputes, and patient cost estimates are all built on bad data. The key changes:

| Change | Old requirement | New requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Reporting frequency | Monthly | Quarterly |
| File location indicator | Not required | Plain-text file at website root pointing to MRF location |
| "Ghost rates" | No explicit standard | Must reflect actual paid rates; executives sign attestation |
| Out-of-network data | Limited | Expanded OON pricing information required |
| Prescription drug files | Voluntary contribution | Schema development begins November 2026; full requirement follows |

The **"ghost rate" problem** has been the biggest practical failure of the original rules: insurers published negotiated rates that looked real but did not reflect actual payments, either because contracts had changed or because the file included rates for providers who had never treated an insured patient. The new attestation requirement — requiring a plan executive to certify the data — is the mechanism CMS is using to force accountability. Practices that see systematic gaps between what a payer's MRF says it pays and what it actually remits now have a documented accuracy standard to cite in disputes.

> **Key Insight:** A practice that runs a competitive market analysis using payer MRFs today is likely working from stale or incorrect data. The December 5 effective date gives payers 57 days to begin correcting their files. The first quarterly submission under the new standard falls in early 2027. Build any rate benchmarking or payer negotiation work on the assumption that the first accurate file won't be available until Q1 or Q2 2027.

### The Fee Schedule Final Rule: What Is Still Unsettled

The July proposed rule contained four decisions that affect day-to-day billing for most practices and that will be locked in by the November final rule:

1. **Conversion factor**: Proposed at **$32.8409** for non-qualifying APM participants (down ~1.68% from 2026) and **$33.1693** for qualifying APM participants (down ~1.19%). The final number may change modestly but is unlikely to turn positive absent legislation.
2. **G2211 modifier**: CMS proposes converting the complexity add-on code into a modifier adding 16% to the value of the billed E/M code, rather than a separate billing unit.
3. **Same-day E/M with global procedures**: Proposed 50% reduction for a separately identifiable office E/M billed with a 0-, 10-, or 90-day global procedure on the same day.
4. **MIPS sunset**: CMS proposes ending traditional MIPS reporting in **2029**, accelerating transition to APMs.

The **340B mandatory reporting** proposal — requiring practices to report claims data to the 340B claims repository — also closes the comment period in November.

> **Warning:** With federal funding expiring **December 11** and the fee schedule final rule expected in November, the last six weeks of 2026 are the highest-density regulatory period practices will face before the January 1, 2027 go-live date for electronic prior authorization, the GLOBE model, and a new conversion factor. Build your 2027 budget line for Medicare revenue before the final rule lands, and revise it within 48 hours of publication.

## Section 02 — Prior Authorization / Payer Relations

### Open Enrollment Opens in Six Days: UHC's 1,700 Prior Authorization Removals Hit Week Two, Electronic PA API Goes Live January 1, and At Least One State Now Bars Insurers From Making Coverage Decisions by AI Alone

Medicare open enrollment opens **October 15** — six days from today — and runs through **December 7**. Your front-desk staff will be answering benefit questions in the middle of two parallel payer shifts: UnitedHealthcare's October 1 prior authorization removals are now in their second week, and the **federal electronic prior authorization API deadline** is 84 days away.

**Stat cards:**
- **October 15** — First day of Medicare open enrollment 2027
- **~1,700** — Codes removed from UHC prior authorization requirements effective October 1, 2026
- **84 days** — Until electronic PA API requirements take effect (January 1, 2027)
- **44%** — Practices reporting prior authorization turnaround is slower in 2026 vs. 2025 (MGMA)

### UHC Week Two: What to Watch for on Remits

UnitedHealthcare's October 1 change removed prior authorization requirements on approximately 1,700 codes across commercial, Medicare Advantage, Community Plan, Individual Exchange, and Oxford plan lines. The affected clinical areas include cardiology imaging and interventional procedures, oncology, orthopedics, genetic and lab testing, chiropractic, and physical and occupational therapy. For practices that saw repeated PA-related claim delays in these categories through September, last week's claims should now be cleaner on the prior-auth front.

Two cautions apply. First, removing PA does not remove all claim review: UHC retains post-payment review rights, and a code removed from PA may still trigger concurrent or retrospective utilization management. Second, plan-level variants exist — the authorization requirement for a specific code may differ across Oxford vs. a commercial UHC plan vs. a Medicare Advantage product. Verify each line on the payer's official code list, not the summary coverage.

**Fidelis Care** also updated its prior authorization requirements effective October 1, including some removals based on clinical review criteria, per its October 2026 provider bulletin.

### The January 1, 2027 Electronic PA Clock

As of **January 1, 2027**, Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid managed care, and CHIP managed care organizations that are subject to the CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization rule (CMS-0057-F) must support the **Prior Authorization API** — a FHIR-based electronic connection that lets EHR and practice management systems send and receive prior authorization requests without navigating individual payer portals. **61% of practices currently access seven or more payer portals every week** (MGMA); the API is designed to collapse that to a single workflow.

In practice, implementation varies. The rule requires the payer to build the API; it does not require the provider to use it. A practice that uses an EHR or clearinghouse that has integrated the API by January 1 gets the benefit automatically. A practice whose vendor has not integrated will see no change and should ask their vendor directly about their timeline.

### The State AI-in-PA Question

At least one state — effective **October 1, 2026** — bars health insurers from making coverage determinations based **solely on AI** and requires disclosure to providers when AI is used in a utilization review decision. The law (SB 63, signed April 17, 2026) reflects a growing legislative response to documented cases of automated claim denials that were upheld without individual clinical review. Similar proposals are active in several additional states. The federal equivalent — a proposed CMS rule that would require disclosure of AI use in coverage decisions for Medicare Advantage plans — remains pending.

> **Action Required:** Before October 15, prepare a one-page internal reference card listing (a) which payer plans in your market are exiting for 2027, (b) which prior authorization codes UHC removed from your highest-volume categories, and (c) when your clearinghouse or EHR vendor plans to support the January 1 electronic PA API. Give it to both your scheduling team and your billing team — open enrollment questions start this week.

## Section 03 — AI & Automation Deep Dive

### HFMA Draws the Line Between AI and Agentic AI in the Revenue Cycle — as 78% of Health Systems Automate but 59% Have Not Yet Started

On **October 6, 2026**, HFMA hosted a virtual session on determining which revenue cycle work suits **traditional automation**, **agentic AI**, or **human expertise**. The distinction matters because the tools work differently and fail differently — and the spread between organizations that have implemented AI and those that have not is widening fast.

**Stat cards:**
- **78%** — Health systems using automation or AI to speed up manual RCM processes (Guidehouse/HFMA 2026)
- **59%** — Respondents who have not yet implemented AI or automation in the revenue cycle (Guidehouse/HFMA 2026)
- **88%** — RCM leaders who see payer challenges as a top concern (Guidehouse/HFMA survey)
- **~$100B** — Annual revenue cycle labor pool Waystar says it is targeting with agentic automation

### How HFMA Maps the Work

The October 6 framework positions three tiers of automation against the revenue cycle:

- **Traditional automation (RPA/rules-based)**: Repeatable, high-volume, rules-deterministic tasks — claim status checks, eligibility verification, payment posting, statement generation. These tools have been available for years; the ROI is well-established. The question for most practices is not whether to use them but whether they are already in the tools they pay for and not yet turned on.
- **AI/ML models**: Pattern recognition over large datasets — denial prediction, coding accuracy scoring, underpayment detection. Requires training data volume that independent practices often lack on their own. Most practical access is through a billing vendor or clearinghouse that aggregates data across client networks.
- **Agentic AI**: Multi-step reasoning workflows that take action without step-by-step human direction. The practical examples are claim resolution agents that read an EOB, identify the denial reason, pull the supporting documentation, draft and submit the appeal, and log the outcome — all without a human in the loop. This is where the market is headed in 2026–2027 but where most independent practices are not yet operating.

The Guidehouse/HFMA survey numbers reflect a real split: the **78% using automation** skews heavily toward health systems with larger IT budgets and vendor contracts; the **59% who haven't implemented** are disproportionately smaller practices and independent groups.

### MGMA's Starting Point: Revenue Cycle First

MGMA's IT chief Julia Rosen, speaking on the sidelines of the MGMA centennial conference, offered a concrete starting point for practices new to AI: begin in the revenue cycle, not in clinical AI. Denial prediction and coding support are lower regulatory risk than ambient documentation, have faster ROI, and typically live in software the practice already pays for. The guidance echoes a Guidehouse/HFMA theme: the first AI win is usually unlocking a feature inside an existing contract, not buying a new product.

> **Warning:** The 78%/59% data point reflects a measurement problem: organizations that have turned on a single automation feature in their clearinghouse often report "yes" to automation adoption, while organizations that have deployed 20 workflows in production report the same answer. Implementation maturity matters more than adoption rate. Before benchmarking your AI posture against market numbers, count how many workflows in your existing vendor stack are actually active vs. licensed-but-dormant.

## Section 04 — Coding Updates

### Week Two on FY 2027 ICD-10: Reading the First Remit Mismatches, New Hereditary Cancer Syndrome Codes in Effect, and the October 8 MLN Updates RARC and CARC Tables

The October 1 ICD-10 changeover is now in its second week. Payers typically take **30 to 60 days** after a code set update before their edit libraries fully reflect the new files — which means the first wave of denials attributable to the new codes is landing now, and a second wave may still be coming.

**Stat cards:**
- **190** — New FY 2027 ICD-10-CM codes effective October 1, 2026
- **30–60 days** — Typical lag before payer edit libraries fully reflect new code files
- **84 days** — Until CPT 2027 and 2027 HCPCS annual update take effect (January 1)
- **3** — New hereditary cancer syndrome codes replacing a single "genetic susceptibility" code

### The First-Remit Checklist

The most common sources of denial on week-two remits from an October 1 code update:

| Issue | What to look for | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| Deleted code still active in favorites | Rejection citing invalid code on claims dated October 1+ | Replace with the valid FY 2027 code; audit favorites list |
| NCCI v32.3 new bundle | Denial citing bundling that did not exist in September | Check if modifier is appropriate; review edit file |
| MUE unit limit change | Unit-limit denial on a code whose MUE changed October 1 | Verify new MUE; split encounters if clinically appropriate |
| Payer edit library lag | Claim accepted in September, rejected in October | Resubmit after 30 days or call payer for manual override |

### New Hereditary Cancer Syndrome Codes

One of the highest-impact changes for practices running genetic counseling, oncology, GI, or molecular pathology workflows: FY 2027 introduced dedicated ICD-10-CM codes for **Lynch syndrome, familial BRCA1/BRCA2 variants, and Li-Fraumeni syndrome** — syndromes that previously shared a single "genetic susceptibility to malignant neoplasm" code. The new codes carry their own coverage and medical-necessity rules. A claim using the old unspecified code for a service that now has a specific code available will likely face denial or a request for additional documentation.

The specificity also matters for NCD-governed lab services: any molecular or genetic test tied to a hereditary cancer syndrome diagnosis should now use the specific code, not the unspecified code, to satisfy the LCD/NCD diagnosis pairing.

### October 8 MLN: RARC and CARC Updates

The **October 8, 2026 MLN Connects** edition published updates to the **Remittance Advice Remark Codes (RARC)** and **Claim Adjustment Reason Codes (CARC)** — the two-code combination that explains why Medicare denied or adjusted a claim. Any practice using automated denial triage that maps RARC/CARC combinations to workflow routing needs to update its crosswalk. Updated codes are listed in the CMS CARC and RARC files, effective the same date. The MLN edition also reminded providers using EDI clearinghouses to confirm their **HETTS EDI Enrollment email address** by **December 1, 2026** — necessary for receiving electronic Medicare correspondence going forward.

> **Action Required:** Pull all claims submitted October 1 or later and filter for denials that did not appear in September. Group by RARC/CARC pair, then by CPT code. Any cluster involving a code that changed in the October 1 update is a systemic issue — fix the root cause in your charge master or encoder before resubmitting the batch.

### The Self-Pay Problem Is Getting Worse

HFMA's October–November 2026 issue highlights a growing concern: practices deploying patient-facing technology (payment plans, digital statements, cost estimators) are finding it harder to justify those investments purely on short-term financial returns. The underlying driver is plan design — as 2027 MA plans carry higher maximum out-of-pocket limits and Part D deductibles are rising 30% on average for some plans, the amount of money patients owe per encounter is rising, but their ability and willingness to pay is not rising proportionally.

The practical response: a pre-service financial clearance workflow that identifies patients with high expected out-of-pocket before the visit and coordinates a payment plan or financial assistance discussion at check-in. This is the "front-end velocity" approach — rather than chasing a $400 copay after the encounter, collect $50 before and document a plan for the rest.

> **Key Insight:** The difference between a 93% and a 96% net collection rate on a $3 million annual revenue base is $90,000. For most independent practices, that gap is closed not by a new vendor but by fixing three or four specific process failures: claims not submitted within 24 hours, self-pay accounts not touched within 30 days, and secondary claims not filed when primary pays. Run that specific audit before looking at new technology.

## Section 07 — Compliance Corner

### CMS Finalizes Transparency in Coverage Rule Effective December 5 — Payers Must Cut "Ghost Rates," File Quarterly Instead of Monthly, and Have an Executive Sign Off on Accuracy

The Transparency in Coverage Final Rule (CMS 9882-F), released **October 5, 2026**, is primarily a payer compliance requirement — but it creates audit risk and negotiation leverage for provider organizations that understand it. The rule takes effect **December 5, 2026**, 57 days from today.

**Stat cards:**
- **December 5, 2026** — TiC final rule effective date
- **November 2026** — CMS begins developing schema for Prescription Drug File
- **December 1, 2026** — HETTS EDI Enrollment email deadline for Medicare providers
- **$45,000/day** — Maximum penalty for hospital price transparency noncompliance (per CMS guidelines)

### What Payers Must Do

| Requirement | Timeline |
|---|---|
| Switch to quarterly MRF reporting | Phases in with December 5 effective date |
| Post plain-text root file linking to MRF location | December 5, 2026 |
| Executive attestation on data accuracy | December 5, 2026 |
| Expand out-of-network pricing data | Phased 2027 |
| Develop Prescription Drug File schema | CMS begins November 2026 |

The **attestation requirement** is the enforcement mechanism: executives who sign false attestations face personal liability under federal false-claims provisions. CMS's stated goal is to eliminate "ghost rates" — rates in MRFs that don't reflect actual payments because the contracts have changed or the provider never actually treated an insured patient.

### How Providers Can Use the New Data

Once accurate quarterly files are available:
- **Rate benchmarking**: Compare your actual paid rates against what a payer publishes for similar providers in your market — systematic underpayment may be visible in the MRF data.
- **Contracting leverage**: A rate that is lower than what the payer publishes for a comparable practice in the same ZIP code is a documented discrepancy in contract negotiations.
- **No-surprise billing compliance**: Accurate payer MRFs reduce the risk of a patient receiving a surprise bill based on an incorrect cost-estimate driven by a ghost rate.

### Hospital Price Transparency Enforcement Update

OIG's active work plan includes a review of CMS's oversight of hospital price transparency compliance. CMS's enforcement toolkit includes written warnings, corrective action plans, and civil monetary penalties up to $45,000 per day for large facilities. Practices with a hospital outpatient department component should verify their standard charge files are current and accessible at the required website location before year-end.

## Section 08 — Independent Practice Watch

### 94% Were Approached About Acquisition in Two Years — and the 2027 Proposed Fee Schedule Gives Office-Based Practices the Only Bright Spot in the Payment Calendar

The financial pressure on independent practices in 2026 is structural, not cyclical — and a new Veradigm survey puts numbers on it. But the 2027 proposed fee schedule contains a rarely acknowledged offset that specifically favors independent, office-based practices.

**Stat cards:**

### The Acquisition Pressure

Veradigm's March 2026 survey found that **94%** of independent practices report being approached about acquisition or consolidation in the past two years. Of those, **26%** advanced to serious negotiations before choosing to remain independent. The most significant threats to long-term independence, per respondents: **increased operating costs**, **administrative and regulatory burden**, and **reimbursement pressure**. Only **24%** of practices reported clear visibility into where revenue leaks — a foundational gap that makes financial comparisons with acquiring entities difficult.

For practices with **more than 40 clinicians**, the strain is highest: **82%** report increased financial pressure, and the administrative-to-clinical staff ratio at larger independent groups approaches that of health system-employed practices without the corresponding infrastructure investment.

### The 2027 Offset That Favors Independent Practices

The proposed 2027 fee schedule contains a site-of-service adjustment that most commentary has overlooked: CMS estimates that its proposed methodology changes would increase Medicare payments for services delivered in **independent office-based settings by approximately 4%**, while decreasing payments for the same services delivered in **facility-based (outpatient hospital or HOD) settings by approximately 7%**. This reflects CMS's ongoing effort to reduce the facility differential that has made hospital employment of physicians economically attractive.

The implication: a practice that has resisted acquisition and maintained its independent, office-based billing identity is positioned to benefit from this adjustment — if the final rule retains it. The comment period closed September 14. The final rule lands in November.

> **Bottom Line:** Independence has a material financial value in 2027 if the fee schedule adjustment survives the final rule. The question is whether that value is visible on your P&L — and whether your revenue cycle operations are clean enough to capture it. A practice with a 92% net collection rate on a $3M base loses $90,000 annually compared to a practice at 95%. Fixing that gap is worth more than the site-of-service differential.
